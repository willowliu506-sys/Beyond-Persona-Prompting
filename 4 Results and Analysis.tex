\section{RQ1---Cognitive-Mechanism Effects}
\label{sec:rq1-results}

\subsection{RQ1 Results Overview}
\label{subsec:rq1-overview}

Study 1 addressed RQ1 by testing whether CPB produced correct, attributable, and traceable information-state changes under the predefined processing logic. The analysis followed three linked questions. First, it examined whether the Attention and Working-Memory mechanisms executed their registered rules correctly. Second, it tested whether the resulting changes occurred at the intended processing transitions. Third, it assessed whether these local changes accumulated through the complete processing chain to the Explicit LTM and downstream question performance.

The results are therefore reported in SQ1--SQ3 order. Section~\ref{subsec:rq1-sq1} reports mechanism-execution fidelity; Section~\ref{subsec:rq1-sq2} examines the stage-specific transitions $S_r\rightarrow P_{r,c}\rightarrow A_{r,c}\rightarrow L_{r,c}$; and Section~\ref{subsec:rq1-sq3} evaluates cumulative propagation to the Explicit LTM and final assessment performance. Section~\ref{subsec:rq1-summary} then integrates these three layers of evidence to answer RQ1.

\subsection{SQ1---Mechanism Execution Fidelity}
\label{subsec:rq1-sq1}

SQ1 examined whether the Attention and Working-Memory mechanisms followed their preregistered computational rules. Four implementation-fidelity measures were used. Attention Target-Removal Fidelity (ATRF) checked whether the target sentences scheduled for Attention removal were removed correctly, while Non-Target Preservation Rate (NTPR) checked whether instructional information that should remain unaffected was preserved. Working-Memory Trigger Agreement (WMTA) compared the observed WM trigger status with the frozen processing-demand--capacity rule. FIFO Rule Fidelity (FRF) checked whether a triggered WM constraint removed the earliest instructional sentence that remained available after Attention, as specified by the within-round FIFO-style rule. Values closer to 1 indicate stronger agreement between observed execution and the registered mechanism rules.

Attention execution matched the frozen rules exactly. In each Attention-ON condition, the same 42 eligible distractor-linked target sentences were identified and removed, yielding 84 condition--target removal events across A1W0 and A1W1; ATRF was therefore 1.000 in both conditions. All non-target instructional information was preserved in every condition, giving an NTPR of 1.000 throughout. No Attention-attributed target removal occurred under A0W0 or A0W1. The Attention Filter therefore removed the intended information when enabled without introducing unintended non-target deletion.

Working-Memory execution was also fully consistent with the frozen rules. Observed WM trigger status agreed with the registered $D(R_r)>C^{WM}_c$ rule in all 83 Teaching Rounds under every condition, giving a WMTA of 1.000 throughout. In each WM-ON condition, the same 32 Teaching Rounds exceeded the registered capacity threshold, yielding 64 condition--round overflow events across A0W1 and A1W1. The mechanism removed the earliest post-Attention instructional sentence in every eligible event, so FRF was 1.000 in both WM-ON conditions. No capacity-related sentence removal occurred under A0W0 or A1W0.

No implementation violation was observed in any Study 1 condition. Attention triggering, target localisation, and non-target preservation, together with WM threshold triggering and FIFO-style restriction, all achieved complete fidelity. SQ1 was therefore supported: both manipulated CPB mechanisms executed the registered design, providing a valid implementation basis for interpreting the information-state differences reported below. These results establish computational execution fidelity; they do not by themselves establish fidelity to human ADHD cognition.

\subsection{SQ2---Stage-Specific Information-State Transitions}
\label{subsec:rq1-sq2}

Having established correct mechanism execution, SQ2 examined whether the resulting information changes occurred at the intended processing stages:

\[
S_r
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Attention}}
P_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{WM}}
A_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Encoding}}
L_{r,c}.
\]

Three stage-specific measures were used. Attention-stage Availability Ratio (AAR) represents the proportion of Source words retained in the Post-Attention Input. Working-Memory-stage Availability Ratio (WMAR) represents the proportion of Post-Attention Input words that remain available to the Encoder after WM processing. Values closer to 1 indicate less information restriction at the corresponding stage. Encoding Semantic Recall (ESR) uses BERTScore Recall under the frozen configuration to quantify semantic correspondence between each non-empty Available Input and its round-level Explicit LTM. Higher ESR indicates stronger conditional semantic coverage of the information that actually reached the Encoder; it is not a retention proportion relative to the complete Source.

Table~\ref{tab:rq1-stage-measures} reports unweighted means across the seven teaching materials and 95\% lesson-bootstrap intervals. Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-stage-transitions} shows the lesson-level observations, condition means, and corresponding intervals.

The Attention stage showed the expected localisation pattern. AAR was 1.000 under both Attention-OFF conditions, indicating that no Source information became unavailable at this stage. Enabling Attention reduced mean AAR to 0.768 under both A1W0 and A1W1. Switching the downstream WM setting while holding Attention constant produced no lesson-level change in AAR. Attention-attributed information loss was therefore localised to the $S_r\rightarrow P_{r,c}$ transition and was invariant to the downstream WM setting.

The WM stage showed a similarly clear stage-specific pattern. WMAR remained 1.000 under A0W0 and A1W0, indicating that no additional information restriction occurred after Attention when the capacity constraint was disabled. Under WM ON, mean WMAR was 0.806 for A0W1 and 0.783 for A1W1. Additional information loss therefore appeared only when the WM constraint was enabled and was localised to the $P_{r,c}\rightarrow A_{r,c}$ transition. A0W1 and A1W1 were not expected to have identical WMAR values because the Attention manipulation changed the set of instructional sentences available for the subsequent within-round restriction.

Across the shared Encoding stage, mean ESR ranged from 0.798 to 0.831, with substantial overlap among the lesson-bootstrap intervals. No clear condition-specific Encoding divergence was observed. The descriptively highest ESR occurred under A1W1, but its reference was the condition-specific Available Input rather than the complete Source. It therefore cannot be interpreted as evidence of better overall retention of the original instructional material. The observed similarity also does not establish statistical or practical equivalence among conditions.

Together, the SQ2 results show that each manipulated mechanism changed information availability at its intended position: Attention-related loss was localised to $S_r\rightarrow P_{r,c}$, and WM-related loss occurred subsequently at $P_{r,c}\rightarrow A_{r,c}$. The shared Encoder maintained broadly similar semantic correspondence with the information that actually reached it. SQ2 was therefore supported at the level of computational stage-specific traceability; broader cognitive interpretation is reserved for Chapter~\ref{Chap5}.

\subsection{SQ3---Cumulative and Downstream Propagation}
\label{subsec:rq1-sq3}

After SQ2 had localised the Attention, WM, and shared Encoding transitions, SQ3 examined whether these changes accumulated through the complete processing chain. The analysis considered three outcome levels: cumulative Source availability at the Encoder, end-to-end semantic correspondence between the complete Source and Explicit LTM, and downstream assessment performance.

Source-Word Availability Ratio (SWAR) represents the proportion of Source words that reach the Encoder after both Attention and WM. Under the same lesson-level aggregation,

\[
SWAR_{l,c}=AAR_{l,c}\times WMAR_{l,c}.
\]

Lower SWAR therefore indicates stronger cumulative upstream information restriction. End-to-End Semantic Recall (E2ESR) uses the complete Source as the BERTScore reference and the round-level Explicit LTM as the candidate, describing their semantic correspondence without treating the score as a percentage of human knowledge retention. Checklist Score converts the proportion of frozen criteria labelled \texttt{Correct} to a 0--10 scale and measures the downstream consequence of the complete learning pipeline.

Table~\ref{tab:rq1-cumulative-outcomes} reports the unweighted means across the seven teaching materials and 95\% lesson-bootstrap intervals. Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-cumulative-outcomes} presents SWAR, E2ESR, and Checklist Score using the same lesson-level observational units.

SWAR showed cumulative information restriction. Mean SWAR was 1.000 under A0W0, indicating that the complete Source reached the Encoder when both mechanisms were disabled. It decreased to 0.768 when Attention alone was enabled and to 0.806 when WM alone was enabled. With both mechanisms enabled, A1W1 decreased further to 0.601. Across all lesson-level observations, the maximum numerical discrepancy in $SWAR=AAR\times WMAR$ was $1.11\times10^{-16}$, confirming the identity to numerical precision. The stage-specific transitions identified under SQ2 therefore accumulated in the predefined processing order to determine the final Available Input.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-word-decomposition} presents the same cumulative process using pooled absolute word counts. Every condition began with the same 3,719 frozen Source words. All 3,719 words reached the Encoder under A0W0. Under A1W0, Attention removed 868 words and left 2,851 available. Under A0W1, WM removed 717 words and left 3,002 available. Under A1W1, Attention first removed 868 words and WM subsequently removed 603 words, leaving 2,248 words available. Every condition satisfied

\[
W_{\mathrm{source},c}
=
W_{\mathrm{available},c}
+W_{\mathrm{attention\text{-}loss},c}
+W_{\mathrm{WM\text{-}loss},c}.
\]

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-word-decomposition} uses pooled corpus counts, whereas SWAR in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-cumulative-outcomes} is the unweighted mean of seven lesson-level ratios. Consequently, the pooled available-word proportion under A1W1 was 60.4\%, compared with a mean lesson-level SWAR of 0.601; the small difference arises from the aggregation level.

Cumulative information restriction was also reflected in semantic correspondence between the Explicit LTM and the complete Source. Mean E2ESR was 0.798 under A0W0, decreased to 0.638 under A1W0 and 0.679 under A0W1, and was lowest under A1W1 at 0.522. This pattern followed the reduction in upstream Source availability. Combined with the broadly similar ESR values in SQ2, the result indicates that condition-level Source-to-LTM differences primarily accompanied changes in the information reaching the Encoder rather than a condition-specific Encoding procedure.

Downstream assessment performance changed in the same direction. Mean Checklist Score was 9.286 under A0W0, 7.143 under A0W1, 2.891 under A1W0, and 2.415 under A1W1. Descriptively, the Attention-related decrement was larger than the WM-related decrement, and A1W1 produced the lowest mean score. Checklist Score is used here as supporting downstream evidence that upstream information restriction affected final answers, not as the primary test of mechanism-execution fidelity.

Table~\ref{tab:rq1-factorial-contrasts} reports the $2\times2$ factorial contrasts for SWAR, E2ESR, and Checklist Score. The Attention and WM rows are marginal effects averaged over the other factor; the interaction is the difference-in-differences; and the joint contrast compares A1W1 directly with A0W0. A positive interaction here does not mean that jointly enabling both constraints improved information availability or performance. SQ3 was therefore supported. Attention- and WM-related changes localised under SQ2 accumulated in the information reaching the Encoder and were reflected in Source-to-LTM semantic correspondence and final Checklist Score. Study 1 consequently established a traceable chain from local information-state transitions to downstream outcomes.

\subsection{RQ1 Summary}
\label{subsec:rq1-summary}

RQ1 imposed three progressive requirements: correct execution of the Attention and Working-Memory mechanisms, localisation of their effects to the intended processing stages, and propagation of these local effects to the Explicit LTM and downstream performance. Table~\ref{tab:rq1-summary} summarises the corresponding evidence.

First, SQ1 established the implementation foundation for the remaining analyses. All applicable fidelity measures were 1.000 and no mechanism violation occurred. The Attention and WM trigger, target-selection, and removal rules therefore operated as frozen, so the subsequent condition differences need not be explained by implementation error.

Second, SQ2 localised the effects of the two manipulated mechanisms. Attention-related information loss occurred at $S_r\rightarrow P_{r,c}$, while additional WM-related restriction occurred at $P_{r,c}\rightarrow A_{r,c}$. ESR remained within a relatively narrow descriptive range across the four conditions, and no clear condition-specific Encoding divergence was observed. Study 1 therefore identified not only different information states but also the transition at which each difference first arose.

Third, SQ3 showed downstream propagation. Relative to A0W0, the A1W1 joint contrasts were $-0.399$ for SWAR, $-0.276$ for E2ESR, and $-6.871$ for Checklist Score. Cumulative upstream restriction was therefore reflected in the Source information reaching the Encoder, semantic correspondence between the complete Source and final Explicit LTM, and downstream assessment performance. Checklist Score remains supporting behavioural evidence and does not replace the mechanism-execution and stage-localisation evidence.

RQ1 can consequently be answered affirmatively within the scope of the frozen materials, parameters, and operational rules: the CPB processing pipeline executed its predefined logic and produced attributable information-state changes that could be traced through Source, Attention, WM, Encoding, Explicit LTM, and Answer stages. The result supports computational mechanistic validity and process traceability at the implementation level. It does not establish biological equivalence with human ADHD cognitive processes. Having established this mechanism foundation, Section~\ref{sec:rq2-results} compares the behavioural patterns produced by CPB and persona prompting under controlled learning conditions.

\section{RQ2---CPB versus Persona-Prompted ADHD Simulation}
\label{sec:rq2-results}

\subsection{RQ2 Results Overview}
\label{subsec:rq2-overview}

Study 2 addressed RQ2 by comparing whether persona prompting and CPB produced different behavioural sensitivities to controlled distraction and instructional processing demand. The analysis comprised seven learner conditions: Prompt-NT, Moderate-Intensity Prompt-ADHD, High-Intensity Prompt-ADHD, and CPB Zero, Low, Medium, and High. Each learner condition was run independently five times under both clean and distracted materials, producing

\[
7\times2\times5=70
\]

learner--material runs. Every run answered all 49 questions across the seven lessons, yielding 3,430 answers and Judge scores. For each learner condition, material condition, and question, Mean Checklist Score, run-to-run SD, and mean response word count were first calculated across the five runs, producing 686 question-level condition estimates. Of the 49 questions, 42 independent questions had a unique source-round Processing Demand Bits (PDB) value and were used for SQ4. The remaining seven integrative questions were retained as a common question-type dimension for SQ1--SQ3 but were excluded from the continuous PDB analysis.

The results are reported through four progressive subquestions. Section~\ref{subsec:rq2-sq1} examines whether the three Prompt learners produced distinguishable performance as ADHD-description intensity increased. Section~\ref{subsec:rq2-sq2} tests whether the four CPB conditions produced a graded response as registered constraint severity increased. Section~\ref{subsec:rq2-sq3} compares the distraction sensitivity of the two representations using within-condition clean--distracted differences. Section~\ref{subsec:rq2-sq4} then examines the relationship between question performance and source-round PDB. Section~\ref{subsec:rq2-summary} integrates the four sets of findings to answer RQ2 directly.

\subsection{SQ1---Persona Differentiation}
\label{subsec:rq2-sq1}

SQ1 tested whether Prompt-NT, Moderate-Intensity Prompt-ADHD, and High-Intensity Prompt-ADHD produced behavioural differentiation corresponding to persona intensity. The primary outcome was the five-run question-level Mean Checklist Score. Mean question-level SD described the stability of repeated answers to the same question, while mean answer word count was retained only as a descriptive response characteristic. Ordering Consistency Rate (OCR) recorded the proportion of questions satisfying

\[

\geq
\bar S_{\mathrm{Moderate}}
\geq
\bar S_{\mathrm{High}},

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:rq2-persona-performance} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-persona-differentiation}, all three Prompt conditions performed close to the upper boundary of the scoring scale. \subsection{SQ3---Distraction Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:rq2-sq3}

SQ3 used paired performance under clean and distracted materials to calculate distraction cost for each learner condition:

\[
\Delta D_{q,c}
=
\bar S_{q,c,\mathrm{clean}}
-
\bar S_{q,c,\mathrm{distracted}}.
\]

Positive values indicate lower performance under distracted materials. Table~\ref{tab:rq2-distraction-cost} reports the mean distraction cost across the 49 questions, its 95\% confidence interval, and the mean change in question-level SD and response word count from clean to distracted materials.

\subsection{SQ4---Processing-Demand Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:rq2-sq4}

The primary measure was the linear change in Checklist Score for each 100-bit increase in PDB, $\beta_{\mathrm{PDB},100}$. Spearman's $\rho$ described the corresponding rank association without assuming linearity. The Low--High gap was the mean score on the predefined low-demand questions minus the mean score on the high-demand questions. Negative slopes and correlations, together with positive Low--High gaps, indicate that higher processing demand was associated with lower performance.

\subsection{RQ2 Summary}
\label{subsec:rq2-summary}

RQ2 comprised four questions. These examined persona differentiation, graded CPB response, sensitivity to controlled distraction, and systematic sensitivity to instructional processing demand. Table~\ref{tab:rq2-summary} summarises the resulting evidence.

SQ1 showed that Moderate- and High-Intensity ADHD descriptions did not produce a substantively sized performance differentiation among the near-ceiling Prompt responses. SQ2 showed that registered CPB constraint severity produced a clear aggregate score gradient under both material conditions, although stochastic trajectories caused some question-level departures from the expected ordering. SQ3 further showed that clean--distracted differences were close to zero for Prompt learners, whereas distraction cost increased progressively across CPB Low, Medium, and High. Finally, SQ4 showed a stable negative relationship between constrained CPB performance and source-round PDB, while the Prompt conditions and CPB Zero did not exhibit the same pattern.

RQ2 can therefore be answered affirmatively within the scope of the frozen materials, student model, assessment questions, and scoring procedure: compared with persona prompting, CPB produced learning behaviour that responded more systematically and directionally consistently to controlled distraction and instructional processing demand, with those responses corresponding to the registered constraint parameters. This conclusion does not depend on CPB producing lower absolute scores. It rests on the combined evidence of a graded constraint response, clean--distracted sensitivity, and PDB-related performance patterns. The findings support the comparative representational validity of CPB in this experiment, but do not establish clinical or biological equivalence with real learners with ADHD.
