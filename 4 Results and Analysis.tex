\section{RQ1---CPB Mechanism Validation}
\label{sec:rq1-results}

\subsection{SQ1---Mechanism Execution}
\label{subsec:rq1-sq1}

SQ1 examined whether the Attention and Working-Memory mechanisms executed strictly according to their preregistered rules. Four implementation-fidelity measures were used. Attention Target-Removal Fidelity (ATRF) checked whether the target sentences that should have been removed by Attention were deleted correctly; Non-Target Preservation Rate (NTPR) checked whether instructional information that should not have been affected by Attention was preserved correctly. Working-Memory Trigger Agreement (WMTA) checked whether the observed WM triggers agreed with the frozen processing-demand--capacity rule; FIFO Rule Fidelity (FRF) checked whether, after triggering, WM removed the earliest instructional sentence that remained available after Attention, as specified by the predefined within-round FIFO-style rule. Table~\ref{tab:rq1-mechanism-fidelity} summarises the implementation-fidelity results and observed violations across the four Study 1 conditions.

As Table~\ref{tab:rq1-mechanism-fidelity} shows, every applicable implementation-fidelity measure was 1.000 and no implementation violation was observed. In each Attention-ON condition, all 42 frozen eligible targets were removed correctly and no non-target information was removed accidentally. In each WM-ON condition, the same 32 high-demand Teaching Rounds triggered the WM restriction under the registered rule, and the FIFO-style removal was executed correctly. No corresponding unintended removal occurred when Attention or WM was disabled. SQ1 was therefore supported: both mechanisms executed their frozen rules correctly, providing a reliable implementation basis for the subsequent information-state analysis.

The stage-specific results showed a clear localisation pattern. AAR was 1.000 when Attention was OFF and fell to 0.768 when Attention was ON, irrespective of the WM setting, indicating that Attention-related loss was consistently localised to $S_r\rightarrow P_{r,c}$. The stage-specific evidence for SQ2 consequently shows that the information changes produced by Attention and WM occurred at their intended stages, while the shared Encoder maintained descriptively similar semantic correspondence with the Available Input it actually received. The following analysis examines whether these local changes propagated to the resulting Explicit LTM.

In A1W1, Attention and WM removed 868 and 603 words sequentially, leaving 2,248 words; in every condition, the available words and the two attributed losses reconstructed the Source total completely. The cumulative change was also reflected in embedding-based Source-to-LTM correspondence. E2ESR fell from 0.798 in A0W0 to 0.638 in A1W0 and 0.679 in A0W1, and fell further to 0.522 in A1W1. Together with the descriptively similar ESR values, this decline is consistent with reduced upstream information availability. Because no dedicated between-condition Encoding contrast was performed, however, it does not establish statistical equivalence of Encoder behaviour. E2ESR also uses neither proposition extraction nor entailment checking; the values therefore cannot demonstrate preservation of specific propositions, relation directions, or qualifiers, and indicate only that overall contextual-embedding correspondence declined alongside upstream restriction.

SQ2 was therefore supported. Attention, WM, and Encoding transitions could be localised separately; the local Attention- and WM-related information changes accumulated in Available Input and remained reflected in semantic correspondence between the final Explicit LTM and complete Source. This conclusion is not extended to proposition-level knowledge retention.

Taken together, RQ1 shows that the CPB processing pipeline executed according to its predefined rules and produced stage-localised information-state changes that propagated to the final Explicit LTM representation. This supports the mechanistic traceability of the implemented pipeline, while not implying proposition-level knowledge retention or equivalence with human ADHD cognitive processes.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-distracted-scores} and Table~\ref{tab:rq2-distracted-performance} show distinct within-representation patterns. Prompt condition bars nearly overlapped across L01--L07, with lesson means between 9.429 and 10.000 and overall means of 9.871, 9.864, and 9.837. The largest separation was only 0.034 points; persona intensity therefore did not produce performance differentiation of a clear magnitude that was consistent across materials. In contrast, CPB Zero, Low, Medium, and High decreased successively in every lesson, with overall means of 9.374, 6.517, 4.680, and 3.014. The Zero--High difference of approximately 6.36 points demonstrates a clear graded pattern across materials.

Absolute scores also reflect different answer-information sources. Prompt learners could access the complete accumulated classroom context, whereas CPB learners could use only Explicit LTM formed after Attention, WM, and Encoding. Information not processed or retained could not be recovered during CPB answering. The Prompt ceiling may therefore partly reflect continuing full-context availability, while CPB scores reflect both explicit-memory representation and constraint-related information loss.

Overall Mean Question-Level Run SD was low for all Prompt conditions (0.161, 0.000, and 0.129), but must be interpreted alongside their ceiling scores, which left little opportunity for score variation. It demonstrates repeatability of the observed scores, not stronger behavioural stability of persona simulation by itself. CPB Zero was also low at 0.262, while Low, Medium, and High rose to 1.967, 2.388, and 1.853. Variability did not increase monotonically with severity. The maximum at Medium is consistent with maximum Bernoulli variance at an Attention probability of 0.50 and a central WM threshold, but the present analysis does not isolate the causal contribution of either mechanism. Run SD remains descriptive rather than a direct measure of performance ordering or ADHD-mechanism fidelity.

Prompt answers were markedly longer than CPB answers: overall means ranged from 80.84 to 86.07 words for Prompt and from 33.46 to 43.43 for CPB. Within both representations, answers generally lengthened as experimental intensity increased. Prompt means were 80.84, 81.41, and 86.07, suggesting that higher persona intensity was associated mainly with longer output while NT and Moderate remained close. CPB means were 33.46, 39.88, 42.91, and 43.43; answers did not shorten as available memory decreased.

For Independent Questions, Prompt scores were 9.865, 10.000, and 9.984 with 68.83, 74.46, and 79.75 words; output length increased without a performance gradient. For Integrative Questions, scores were 9.905, 9.048, and 8.952 with 152.86, 123.11, and 124.00 words, showing larger descriptive separation. \section{RQ3---Multidimensional Representation Performance}
\label{sec:rq3-results}

\subsection{SQ1---Baseline-Relative Behavioural Change}
\label{subsec:rq3-sq1}

Using distracted materials only, SQ1 examined how behavioural outcomes changed relative to matched Study 2 baselines after adding the A1--B2 profile dimensions, and whether the original within-representation performance structure remained identifiable. Learner-condition results first locate the sources of overall score change. Run SD and Mean Answer Words then describe stability and response form, while Lesson/Question OCR, ER, and score separation assess retention of performance structure. Representation-by-profile means, matched baselines, and deltas are reported together in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix-study3-outcome-changes}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter4/Study3_v3_Figure_1_SQ1_distracted_mean_score_delta.png}
\caption{Learner-condition Mean Checklist Score changes relative to matched Study 2 distracted-material baselines. Positive values indicate higher Study 3 performance.}
\label{fig:rq3-score-delta}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq3-score-delta} shows that the same A1--B2 additions affected Prompt and CPB differently, especially for A2 and B2. A1 (High Language Ability; High O, High C, Low N) had limited effects: Prompt Moderate changed by $+0.014$, Prompt High by $-0.177$, and CPB Low, Medium, and High by $+0.197$, $+0.082$, and $-0.088$. Representation-level changes were only $-0.082$ for Prompt and $+0.063$ for CPB. A2 (Low Language Ability; Low O, Low C, High N) lowered both Prompt conditions by approximately 0.952 points, but increased CPB Low, Medium, and High by 0.544, 0.231, and 0.265, giving a CPB representation-level change of $+0.347$. Thus, aligned profiles did not produce a common cross-representation effect.

For B1 (High Language Ability; Low O, Low C, High N), both Prompt conditions fell by approximately 0.388, while CPB Low, Medium, and High changed by $-0.088$, $+0.054$, and $-0.177$; representation means were $-0.388$ and $-0.070$. Under B2 (Low Language Ability; High O, High C, Low N), Prompt Moderate and High fell by 0.551 and 0.857 while CPB levels increased by 0.646, 0.456, and 0.571, producing opposite representation-level changes of $-0.704$ and $+0.558$. Comparing A1 with B2 and A2 with B1 suggests stronger Prompt score changes with Language Ability direction. CPB did not show the same pattern: both Low Language profiles produced positive score displacement. The influence of a profile therefore depended on its interaction with representation architecture.

Score delta alone cannot determine whether within-representation structure was retained or whether changes arose from stability or response form. \section{Summary of Findings and Discussion}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

Across the three research questions, the central issue was not whether CPB merely produced larger score differences, but whether persona prompting and process-based representation produced qualitatively different simulated-student behaviour. Persona prompting specified learner identity and behaviour through explicit descriptions; CPB operationalised selected ADHD-related characteristics as Attention and Working-Memory constraints, allowing them to change available information and Explicit LTM before influencing assessment responses.

RQ1 showed that Attention and WM executed their frozen rules and produced localised information loss at the intended processing stages. These restrictions jointly determined Available Input and remained reflected in semantic correspondence between Source and final Explicit LTM, while the shared Encoder maintained descriptively similar correspondence with the information it actually received. CPB therefore formed a learner-specific knowledge state with an explicit processing history. This validates implementation fidelity and traceability of bounded functional abstractions, not equivalence with human ADHD cognition.

RQ2 showed that CPB more consistently produced task-specific behaviour in the predicted direction of the target ADHD-related hypotheses. Prompt-ADHD showed neither stable additional distraction costs nor clear negative PDB--performance relationships relative to Prompt-NT. Constrained CPB learners showed severity-related distraction loss and lower performance with higher PDB once constraints were enabled. Prompt remained close to ceiling with little persona-intensity separation, whereas CPB retained a graded cross-material pattern. Because PDB participates in the WM rule, these findings evidence propagation of the processing-demand constraint rather than an independent validation of human WM demand.

The comparison also reflects two complete simulation architectures. Persona characteristics enter response-generation context directly and Prompt learners can re-access the full teaching context. CPB does not prescribe a task outcome directly; consequences emerge after Attention and WM alter information availability and Explicit LTM, which then uniquely constrains answering. Prompt ceilings may partly reflect full-context access, while CPB information loss persists. Differences cannot therefore be attributed solely to persona-versus-process representation.

OCR and ER reveal different micro--macro structures. Prompt's high question-level ordering was largely tie-driven and did not reliably produce lesson-level separation. CPB showed more item-level reversals but consistent, substantively separated gradients after lesson aggregation: micro-level variability coexisted with macro-level regularity. Without a real-learner benchmark, this cannot establish which structure is closer to human ADHD behaviour.

RQ3 showed that Language Ability and Big-Five additions changed scores, response length, and run-to-run variability without eliminating CPB's graded performance, controlled-distraction sensitivity, or overall negative PDB--performance pattern. Response-stage attributes altered individual outputs while the established constraint-related aggregate structure remained identifiable. This supports pattern retention, not independent fidelity of the added characteristics.

The Main Research Question therefore receives a qualified affirmative answer. CPB produced traceable processing-state changes and more consistent task-specific sensitivity in the predicted directions of controlled distraction and processing demand than persona prompting, and those constraint-related patterns remained observable after additional response-stage attributes were introduced. The evidence supports mechanistic traceability, theory-direction-consistent task sensitivity, and constraint-pattern retention for the operationalised processes; it does not directly validate real ADHD cognition or human behavioural fidelity.
