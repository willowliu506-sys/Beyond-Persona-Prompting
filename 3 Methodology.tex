\section{Research Questions and Overall Experimental Framework}

\subsection{Research Questions and Study Mapping}

The overall research question guiding this study is:

\begin{quote}
\scriptsize
\textbf{Main RQ:} How can selected ADHD-related learning characteristics be represented in LLM-based student simulation, and to what extent does a cognitive-process-based representation produce more theory-consistent task behaviour than persona prompting while retaining its constraint-related patterns under additional response-stage learner attributes?
\end{quote}

This overarching question is addressed through three research questions with distinct evidential roles:

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{RQ1---Mechanism:} Does the CPB processing pipeline execute its intended rules and produce traceable transitions from instructional information, through attention, working-memory processing and encoding, to Explicit LTM?

\textbf{RQ2---Representation:} Compared with persona prompting, does CPB produce learning behaviour that shows more theoretically consistent sensitivity to controlled distraction and instructional processing demand?

\textbf{RQ3---Constraint-Pattern Retention under Attribute Additions:} When Language Ability and Big-Five prompt dimensions are added at the response stage, how do assessment outcomes change, and do CPB's previously established constraint-related aggregate patterns remain observable?
\end{quote}

The three research questions form a progressive evidential sequence. Study 1 first establishes whether the internal CPB mechanisms execute as intended, produce stage-specific information-state changes, and propagate these changes to the final Explicit LTM. Study 2 then compares Prompt-based and CPB-based learner representations in terms of within-representation performance differentiation and behavioural sensitivity to controlled distraction and instructional processing demand. Study 3 introduces Language Ability and Big-Five prompt dimensions at the response stage, describes the resulting outcome changes, and checks whether the constraint-related aggregate patterns previously established for CPB remain observable.

\subsection{Overall Research Workflow}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_overall_research_workflow.png}
    \caption{Overall workflow of the Teaching and Assessment Phases.}
    \label{fig:overall-research-workflow}
\end{figure}

All three studies share a common \textbf{Teaching/Learning Phase} in which instructional materials are processed into a frozen post-exposure information state. Study 1 terminates at the resulting Explicit LTM and validates only the internal mechanism execution and information-state transitions. Studies 2 and 3 additionally proceed to an \textbf{Assessment Phase}, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:overall-research-workflow}, so that the behavioural consequences of the learner representations can be compared without treating answer performance as evidence for RQ1.

During the \textbf{Teaching/Learning Phase}, frozen instructional materials are presented sequentially to the simulated learner and processed according to the corresponding learner representation. This process produces a \textbf{frozen post-exposure information state} that represents the information available after instruction. In Study 1, this state is the terminal analysis object. In Studies 2 and 3, it is frozen before assessment so that subsequent questions do not alter the learned information available to the learner.

For Studies 2 and 3, each \textbf{Assessment Phase} question is answered independently from the same frozen post-exposure state. Each question therefore forms a separate branch from the completed learning phase, preventing earlier questions or generated answers from providing additional information to later questions. The resulting student answers are then evaluated using the same criterion-level scoring framework, providing a common behavioural outcome for the representational comparisons in RQ2 and RQ3.

This design separates \textbf{learner-state formation during instruction} from \textbf{observable assessment behaviour}. RQ1 is restricted to the former and ends at Explicit LTM, whereas RQ2 and RQ3 use both phases to examine assessment behaviour after the learning state has been frozen.

The remainder of Chapter 3 specifies the methodological components required to operationalise and evaluate this framework. Section 3.2 first establishes the shared experimental resources used across all studies, including the instructional materials and preprocessing procedure, the operationalisation of instructional processing demand, the assessment questions, and the criterion-level scoring framework. Section 3.3 then defines the two learner-representation approaches compared in this thesis---persona prompting and the Cognitive-Process-Based (CPB) framework---and clarifies how they differ in learner-characteristic representation, knowledge-state formation, and answer-stage information access. Building on this comparison, Section 3.4 details the CPB mechanisms themselves, including the Attention Filter, Working-Memory processing capacity, and shared knowledge-encoding procedure. Section 3.5 then translates the three research questions into Studies 1--3, specifying the corresponding experimental conditions, comparisons, and evaluation measures for mechanistic validity, comparative representational validity, and multidimensional attribute extension. Finally, Section 3.6 documents the model and generation settings, reproducibility procedures, statistical and repetition conventions, and the ethical scope within which the resulting simulated-learner behaviours are interpreted.

\section{Shared Instructional and Assessment Resources}
\label{sec:teaching-materials-dataset}

\subsection{Source Instructional Materials and Text Preprocessing}
\label{subsec:source-materials}

The instructional corpus comprised seven English-language lessons adapted from Sections 1.1--1.7 of \textit{OpenStax Principles of Finance 2e} \cite{dahlquist2026principlesfinance2e}. The lessons covered the definition and role of finance, the use of data and technology, careers in finance, financial markets and participants, microeconomic and macroeconomic influences, and financial instruments. A single subject area was used to reduce variation attributable to disciplinary genre while retaining a mixture of definitions, factual relations, comparisons, processes, examples, and causal or conditional information suitable for short-answer assessment.

The selected source material was converted into a plain-text instructional format. These sections contained no complex tables or figures, apart from simple process or structural diagrams that could be represented clearly in text. References to source images were replaced with concise textual descriptions of the instructional information they conveyed. This treatment preserved continuity between surrounding passages and avoided leaving incomplete or disconnected content when visual elements were removed from the text-only materials.

The instructional materials and associated assessment resources were finalised and frozen before the formal experiments. The same frozen materials were used across all experimental conditions and repeated runs.

The source summaries were preprocessed before teaching-round segmentation so that each sentence could be interpreted without relying on an antecedent located in a previous round. Context-dependent sentence openings, including unresolved uses of \textit{it}, \textit{they}, and similarly underspecified noun phrases, were replaced with explicit referents where necessary. This operation was restricted to reference resolution: it was not intended to introduce new instructional claims, simplify the underlying finance content, or alter the order in which information was presented.

Following preprocessing, each sentence was reviewed for local interpretability. The review asked whether a student receiving the sentence as part of a short teaching round could identify the subject, relation, and relevant object or outcome without access to an earlier round. The resulting context-independent text formed the clean instructional source from which all experimental conditions were derived.

The preprocessed lessons were divided into teaching rounds containing two or three complete source sentences. Original sentences were not split merely to equalise round length, because syntactic splitting could interrupt a relation or create a fragment that remained difficult to interpret despite coreference resolution. The segmentation procedure instead sought a practical balance between local coherence and moderately comparable round length while preserving source order.

Rounds were assigned identifiers of the form \texttt{R01}, \texttt{R02}, and so forth within each lesson. Sentences were assigned nested identifiers such as \texttt{R01\_S01} and \texttt{R01\_S02}. Each round retained its source sentences in their original order together with a reconstructed instructional text field. The frozen round and sentence identifiers provided a consistent basis for aligning the instructional materials with the experimental resources used in later stages of the study.

\subsection{Instructional Processing-Demand}
\label{subsec:instructional-processing-demand}

This study examines how ADHD-related learner representations process information during controlled classroom learning. The subsequent experiments therefore require a common material-level variable representing the relative processing demand imposed by different Teaching Rounds. This variable serves two functions. First, it provides the shared demand reference for the Working-Memory processing-capacity manipulation described in Section 3.4.2. Second, it provides a continuous material-level variable for analysing whether question performance in Studies 2 and 3 changes systematically with instructional processing demand. A \textbf{Processing Demand Bits (PDB)} value was therefore computed and frozen for every Teaching Round and held constant across all learner conditions.

\subsubsection{Measurement Basis}

Word count alone is insufficient for this purpose. Teaching Rounds of similar length may differ in processing demand because their text differs in predictability; surface length therefore cannot distinguish between instructional inputs that are equally long but differently demanding to process. The present study consequently uses surprisal as the basis of its instructional processing-demand annotation. Surprisal measures how unpredictable a linguistic unit is given its preceding context. For model token $x_t$ at position $t$, surprisal is defined as:

\[
I_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})=-\log_2 P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}).
\]

A lower conditional probability indicates that the token is less predictable from its preceding context and consequently has higher surprisal. Because the logarithm uses base 2, surprisal is measured in bits.

Surprisal has long been used in probabilistic accounts of incremental language processing, where less predictable linguistic input is associated with greater processing difficulty \cite{hale-2001-probabilistic,smith-levy-2013-effect}. Large-scale analyses across multiple reading datasets and language-model estimators have continued to find systematic relationships between contextual predictability and reading time \cite{shain-etal-2024-large-scale}. These findings provide a basis for using conditional predictability as a computational proxy for the relative processing demand of instructional text.

The measure is suitable for the present study because Teaching Rounds are organised as continuous, locally coherent instructional inputs. Summing token-level surprisal within a Teaching Round retains two material properties relevant to processing demand: the amount of linguistic input in the round and the difficulty of predicting that input from its within-round context. It therefore provides a richer material-side reference than word count alone. Surprisal does not, however, capture every contributor to cognitive demand, including prior knowledge, discourse comprehension, dependency retrieval, or conceptual complexity. PDB is consequently treated as a \textbf{computational proxy for relative instructional processing demand}, rather than as a direct measure of human cognitive load.

\subsubsection{Round-Level Operationalisation}

Each Teaching Round $R_i$ receives an independent PDB value calculated by summing the surprisal of all model tokens in the round:

\[
D_\theta(R_i)=\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}-\log_2 P_\theta\!\left(x_{i,t}\mid \mathrm{BOS},x_{i,1:t-1}\right),
\]

where $T_i$ is the number of model tokens in Teaching Round $R_i$, and $\mathrm{BOS}$ is an unscored boundary token used to initialise the round. The resulting $D_\theta(R_i)$ is measured in bits and stored in the \texttt{processing\_demand\_bits} field of the frozen instructional-material record.

The primary estimator was GPT-2 small, the approximately 124-million-parameter autoregressive language model identified as \texttt{gpt2} on Hugging Face. GPT-2 directly provides the next-token conditional probabilities required to calculate surprisal and uses byte-level byte-pair encoding, thereby avoiding the out-of-vocabulary problem of traditional word-level models. It can also be frozen and run locally, at a computational cost compatible with repeated verification across the complete instructional corpus. The model and tokenizer were fixed to the same archived version; the complete version identifiers and execution environment were recorded in the experimental reproducibility manifest.

PDB was calculated from the clean instructional text of each Teaching Round after coreference resolution. GPT-2 context was reset at the beginning of every round and initialised with an unscored boundary token. Tokens from the two or three sentences within a round remained continuous, allowing a later sentence to use earlier sentences from the same round as context. Previous Teaching Rounds were not included in the probability calculation. The PDB of each Teaching Round was therefore independent of its position within the full lesson and of the learner condition subsequently applied to it.

PDB was calculated offline and frozen before student simulation. For every Teaching Round $R_i$, all learner conditions used the same $D_\theta(R_i)$; it was not recalculated after later processing stages retained or removed information:

\[
R_i \longrightarrow D_\theta(R_i).
\]

Because PDB is cumulative, it necessarily contains a text-length component. Across the 83 frozen Teaching Rounds, GPT-2 PDB and surface word count had a Spearman correlation of $\rho=0.727$. Longer rounds therefore tended to have higher cumulative demand, but the two measures were not equivalent: differences in conditional predictability produced different PDB values among rounds of similar length. Word count and PDB were consequently retained together to describe surface length and language-model-based cumulative processing demand, respectively.

\subsubsection{Estimator-Sensitivity Analysis}

Surprisal is not a textual property that exists independently of its probability model. Models may produce different estimates because they differ in training corpus, tokenisation, context representation, and architecture. To test whether the relative instructional-demand structure used in this study depended excessively on GPT-2, a heterogeneous estimator---a fixed-discount interpolated Kneser--Ney word trigram---was used for sensitivity analysis. The trigram model was trained on the Brown and Reuters corpora and, like GPT-2, reset its context at every Teaching-Round boundary.

Because GPT-2 operates on byte-pair-encoded tokens whereas the Kneser--Ney model operates on word tokens, their raw cumulative bit values are not directly comparable. The sensitivity analysis therefore primarily compared the percentile-rank ordering of the 83 Teaching Rounds under the two estimators and additionally compared estimator-specific Low, Middle, and High quartile groups. Complete results are reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix-pdb-sensitivity}.

The maximum GPT-2 reproduction error for the frozen PDB values was 0.000349 bits, and the maximum range across ten repeated computations was 0 bits, indicating deterministic and highly reproducible implementation. The Teaching-Round orderings produced by GPT-2 and Kneser--Ney were strongly positively associated, with Spearman $\rho=0.864$ and a lesson-stratified bootstrap 95\% confidence interval of $[0.782,0.917]$; within-lesson correlations ranged from $0.782$ to $0.952$. No direct Low-to-High reversal occurred between the two estimators. However, Low-tail and High-tail Jaccard similarity was 0.615 and 0.448, respectively; Low/Middle/High agreement was 68.7\%, with Cohen's $\kappa=0.501$. The overall relative ordering was therefore robust, while exact quartile membership remained sensitive to estimator choice.

GPT-2 small was retained as the formal PDB estimator because it directly outputs autoregressive next-token probabilities, permits the model and tokenizer versions to be fixed locally, produces deterministic repeated scores, avoids traditional word-level out-of-vocabulary problems through byte-level tokenisation, and yields a Teaching-Round ordering that showed strong relative agreement with the heterogeneous Kneser--Ney estimator. This selection does not assume that GPT-2 is the uniquely correct language model or the closest model of human cognition. It treats GPT-2 as a transparent, reproducible measurement instrument that has undergone a cross-estimator sensitivity check. The final PDB value was stored in the \texttt{processing\_demand\_bits} field of each frozen Teaching-Round record and used as a shared, fixed material-level annotation across all subsequent experimental conditions.

\subsection{Question Construction}
\label{subsec:question-construction}

Each lesson was paired with seven short-answer assessment questions: six Independent Questions and one Integrative Question. The seven lessons therefore produced 49 frozen questions, comprising 42 Independent Questions and seven Integrative Questions. Every question was constructed directly from the frozen instructional materials, and its content, source mapping, and reference answer were reviewed and frozen before student simulation began.

An \textbf{Independent Question} assesses knowledge that can be localised to a single Teaching Round. Each Independent Question maps to exactly one \texttt{source\_round\_id}, although its answer evidence may come from one or more source sentences within that round. These questions primarily examine whether the learner retained and can express a local definition, factual set, comparison, causal mechanism, or conceptual relation. Because each question maps uniquely to one Teaching Round, it also inherits that round's frozen \texttt{processing\_demand\_bits} value, supporting the subsequent processing-demand analysis. An \textbf{Integrative Question} requires information from multiple Teaching Rounds to be combined and cannot be answered from a single local sentence. In the frozen question set, each lesson's Integrative Question draws on three or four Teaching Rounds and primarily assesses cross-round relations, contrasts, shared frameworks, or causal chains.

Question construction began with a review of the instructional content at Teaching-Round level. A round entered the Independent-Question candidate set only when it contained information from which a clear question, reference answer, and bounded set of judgeable scoring points could be constructed. The procedure did not require every Teaching Round to produce a question: some rounds primarily provided contextual continuity or contained content that could not be decomposed cleanly into assessment propositions. Question construction therefore prioritised content validity and scoring clarity rather than mechanical coverage of every round.

The frozen questions were assigned to five question types, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:question-type-distribution}.

These question types were selected because their expected answers could be decomposed into a bounded set of semantically explicit propositions traceable to source evidence. Identification and Listing questions, for example, allow separately listed source items to become independently judgeable scoring points. Comparison and Contrast questions allow the defining features of each comparison target to be assessed separately. Relationship Explanation and Cause or Mechanism Explanation questions allow the required entities, direction of relation, and outcome to be evaluated explicitly. Questions did not require verbatim reproduction of the instructional text, but every correct answer had to be supported by a meaning-preserving paraphrase of the frozen source. Criterion construction and scoring are specified in Section 3.2.4.

Candidate questions were further reviewed for question validity, source grounding, and rubric clarity. Only questions whose content and scoring requirements passed this review entered the final frozen selection.

Question-to-source mappings use the identifier system defined in Section 3.2.1. Each question has a unique \texttt{question\_id} and records its \texttt{lesson\_id}, \texttt{question\_scope}, \texttt{question\_type}, \texttt{source\_round\_ids}, \texttt{source\_sentence\_ids}, and \texttt{reference\_answer}. Independent Questions additionally inherit the \texttt{processing\_demand\_bits} value and \texttt{demand\_group} of their unique source Teaching Round.

Independent Questions map to one Teaching Round and its specific source sentences; Integrative Questions list the multiple round and sentence identifiers required to answer them. This structure makes every question and its later scoring criteria traceable to explicit source evidence:

\par\smallskip
Question wording, source mappings, reference answers, and checklist criteria remained frozen during the formal experiments, ensuring that every experimental condition used the same assessment content and scoring basis.

\subsection{Scoring Rubric and Atomic Checklist Criteria}

Following the rubric-based and structured criterion-level evaluation principles reviewed in Section 2.6, each assessment question was represented using a frozen set of independently judgeable, source-grounded criteria rather than a single holistic scoring rubric. The study integrates these principles into the frozen criterion-level scoring pipeline shown in Figure~\ref{fig:criterion-scoring-pipeline}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_criterion_scoring_pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{Frozen criterion-level scoring and validation pipeline.}

In this study, \textit{atomic} is an operational term: each criterion represents one instructional proposition that can be judged independently, rather than a uniquely indivisible linguistic fact. Criteria within the same question were designed to minimise semantic overlap while collectively covering the knowledge required by the reference answer. Each criterion was linked to supporting evidence in the frozen instructional materials, ensuring that the scoring checklist did not introduce requirements beyond the taught source content.

\subsubsection{Rubric Construction and Deterministic Score Calculation}
\label{subsec:rubric-construction}

The scoring rubric and checklist were constructed, reviewed, and frozen before formal student-answer generation and scoring. The LLM Judge cannot generate or modify scoring requirements during evaluation. Each assessment question is stored as a structured record containing a unique question identifier, question scope, question type, question text, reference answer, source Teaching Round, source sentence identifiers, and a checklist of atomic criteria. Each criterion additionally contains a unique \texttt{criterion\_id}, the proposition to be expressed, source sentence identifiers, and the original evidence span supporting that proposition. An independent question corresponds to one frozen Teaching Round, whereas an integrative question requires knowledge from multiple Teaching Rounds. Questions from all seven lessons use the same data structure, and the consistency among questions, reference answers, checklists, source identifiers, and evidence spans was checked before freezing.

During scoring, the LLM Judge receives only the following information:

\[
\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
\text{question ID},\\
\text{question},\\
\text{reference answer},\\
\text{checklist criteria},\\
\text{source-evidence spans},\\
\text{student answer}
\end{array}
\right\}.
\]

The Judge payload excludes learner identity, simulation condition, lesson label, demand group, Processing Demand Bits, memory state, Attention state, WM capacity, and all other experimental metadata. These local metadata are reattached to the scoring output only after the Judge response passes structural validation. This blind evaluation design prevents the Judge from inferring the score that a simulated learner should receive from the expected behaviour of a condition.

The Judge does not directly generate a numerical score. It independently assigns \texttt{Correct}, \texttt{Absent}, or \texttt{Contradicted} to each criterion. For \texttt{Correct} and \texttt{Contradicted}, it must also copy the shortest continuous span from the student answer that supports the judgement; for \texttt{Absent}, the evidence field must be null. The output contract prohibits the Judge from adding, deleting, merging, splitting, or redefining criteria. The Judge is also instructed to disregard answer length, writing style, fluency, grammar, vocabulary, confidence, formatting, and repetition, and to evaluate only the semantic propositions actually asserted by the student.

After the returned criterion identifiers and evidence spans pass local programmatic validation, Python calculates the question score deterministically. For question \(q\), containing \(K_q\) criteria,

\[
\operatorname{Score}_q
=
10
\times
\frac{
\sum_{k=1}^{K_q}
\mathbb{I}\!\left(y_{qk}=\mathrm{Correct}\right)
}{K_q},
\]

where \(y_{qk}\) is the label assigned to criterion \(k\), and \(\mathbb{I}(\cdot)\) is the indicator function. Both \texttt{Absent} and \texttt{Contradicted} contribute zero credit, but their labels are retained for error-type analysis. The procedure produces a standardised question score from 0 to 10 while preserving information about the specific knowledge components that the student retained or omitted.

\subsubsection{Criterion-Level LLM-as-a-Judge Scoring and Validation}
\label{subsec:judge-validation}

Before formal scoring, the study compared two candidate Judge models: \texttt{qwen3.7-max} and \texttt{gpt-5.6-sol}. Both models received the same frozen Judge prompt, assessment question, reference answer, atomic checklist criteria, and student answer, and returned only a \texttt{Correct}, \texttt{Absent}, or \texttt{Contradicted} label for each criterion. The validation dataset comprised the same 49 Pre-Knowledge Baseline answers, including 42 Independent Questions, seven Integrative Questions, and 137 atomic criteria. Each model independently scored all answers three times, producing 147 question-level calls per model and 294 calls in total.

Model selection was first governed by seven repeatability criteria specified before the results were generated: output schema, criterion-set, and evidence-span validity had to equal 1.00; Criterion-Label Unanimity had to be at least 0.95; Pairwise Criterion Agreement had to be at least 0.97; Exact Question-Score Invariance had to be at least 0.90; absolute-agreement $\operatorname{ICC}(A,1)$ had to be at least 0.95; the maximum difference between replicate overall mean scores could not exceed 0.10 points; and the minimum lesson-level Criterion-Label Unanimity had to be at least 0.90. These thresholds assessed only the internal repeatability of each candidate model.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:judge-repeatability-thresholds}, Qwen achieved 1.000 for Criterion-Label Unanimity, Exact Question-Score Invariance, and $\operatorname{ICC}(A,1)$ across the three scoring repetitions. Its Maximum Replicate-Mean Difference was zero, and it passed all seven specified criteria. GPT achieved 0.9781, 0.9388, and 0.9700, respectively, and passed six criteria, but its Maximum Replicate-Mean Difference of 0.2041 exceeded the specified 0.10-point upper bound. GPT's three overall means were 7.925, 8.129, and 7.925, indicating that its residual variation was concentrated in a small number of criterion decisions. This instability was more pronounced for Integrative Questions, where Criterion-Label Unanimity, Exact Question-Score Invariance, and $\operatorname{ICC}(A,1)$ decreased to 0.9524, 0.8571, and 0.9231, respectively; Qwen remained fully invariant within this scope.

To test whether the frozen checklist rubric could be applied consistently by different scorers, the study also conducted validation with two human raters. Human Judge 1 held a bachelor's degree in economics, while Human Judge 2 was a master's student with a computer-science background. Neither rater had participated in constructing the questions, reference answers, or checklist criteria. Both independently applied the same frozen scoring sheet under the same written scoring protocol and operational guidance. For every criterion, each rater viewed the question, criterion description, source evidence, and student answer, and selected \texttt{Correct}, \texttt{Absent}, or \texttt{Contradicted}. During their initial ratings, the two raters did not discuss the cases and could not see either the other rater's labels or the candidate LLM Judges' labels. They did not calculate question scores manually; after criterion labelling, the program aggregated criterion credits and normalised question scores using the rule defined in Section 3.2.4.1.

The two human raters agreed on 132 of the 137 criteria. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:human-model-scoring-agreement}, their criterion-level agreement was 96.35\%, Cohen's $\kappa$ was 0.795, Exact Question-Score Agreement was 89.80\%, and Question-Score MAE was 0.374. Human Judges 1 and 2 produced overall mean scores of 9.014 and 9.116, respectively. The five disagreements occurred in five different questions and all concerned a \texttt{Correct}--\texttt{Absent} boundary; none involved \texttt{Contradicted}.

After the initial independent ratings, the study conducted a structured audit of the five disagreements. The audit compared the relevant question, criterion, source evidence, student answer, and each rater's recorded evidence and justification. Its purpose was to characterise the disagreement rather than to force a consensus label or overwrite either original judgement. The five cases concerned semantic-entailment boundaries: whether all required components had to appear, whether asset allocation sufficiently expressed diversification, whether an implicit contrast expressed an explicit negation, whether specific job titles satisfied a superordinate role category, and whether distributed technical evidence jointly expressed a complete relation. These cases therefore reflected different boundaries for deciding when related information became sufficient to satisfy a criterion, rather than different understandings of the instructional facts. Atomic checklists substantially narrowed the scoring space but did not eliminate uncertainty in natural-language entailment. The complete audit is reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix-human-disagreement-audit}.

The human ratings also revealed substantial upper-end concentration in the validation sample. Human Judge 1 classified 123/137 criteria as \texttt{Correct} and awarded full credit to 37/49 questions; Human Judge 2 classified 124/137 criteria as \texttt{Correct} and awarded full credit to 38/49 questions. Their full-score rates were therefore 75.51\% and 77.55\%. Because high raw agreement partly occurred under an imbalanced label distribution and near-ceiling performance, the analysis also reports chance-corrected Cohen's $\kappa$, Exact Question-Score Agreement, and MAE rather than treating raw agreement alone as sufficient evidence of scoring validity.

No human rater was designated as a unique gold standard. Model--human alignment was therefore calculated separately using each human rater as the reference. \section{Learner Representation: Persona Prompting versus CPB}

Figure~\ref{fig:persona-cpb-learning-path-comparison} presents the two learner-representation pipelines compared in this study: Persona Prompting and the Cognitive-Process-Based (CPB) framework. Both approaches use matched Teaching Rounds and the same assessment and scoring procedures, but they differ systematically in how learner characteristics are represented, how the learning process is structured, what knowledge state is retained after teaching, and which information is available during answer generation.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_persona_cpb_learning_path_comparison.pdf}
    \caption[Persona Prompting and CPB learner comparison.]{Representation-level comparison of Persona Prompting and the CPB learner pipeline.}
    \label{fig:persona-cpb-learning-path-comparison}
\end{figure}

In Persona Prompting, explicit persona descriptions primarily represent the target learner characteristics. Instructional information remains available in the accumulated full teaching context and is used jointly with the persona instructions when an assessment answer is generated. CPB instead operationalises the selected ADHD-related characteristics as functional constraints acting during learning. Instructional information passes through explicit processing stages to form an Explicit Long-Term Memory (LTM), and assessment answers are subsequently restricted to that knowledge state.

The two approaches therefore instantiate different learner representation pipelines. Persona Prompting represents learner characteristics as descriptive instructions that condition generated behaviour directly, while retaining the complete accumulated teaching context as the answer-stage information source. CPB locates the selected cognitive characteristics within the learning process itself: functional constraints alter information availability, the resulting input is encoded into an explicit knowledge state, and that state constrains subsequent response generation.

Beneath this overall architectural distinction, the first difference concerns how instructional information is retained and used after exposure. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:persona-cpb-answer-information-source}, Persona Prompting uses the complete \texttt{[CLASSROOM\_EXPERIENCE]} as the knowledge source for later answers. As Teaching Rounds proceed, the earlier teaching material and corresponding student turns remain accumulated in the conversation context and can be accessed again during assessment. The teaching experience is therefore not transformed into a separate learner-specific knowledge representation; instead, the complete interaction history carries the previously presented instructional information.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_persona_cpb_answer_information_source_comparison.png}
    \caption{Answer-stage information available to Persona Prompting and CPB.}
    \label{fig:persona-cpb-answer-information-source}
\end{figure}

CPB, by contrast, produces an explicit \texttt{[LEARNED\_MEMORY]} during the Teaching Phase. In each Teaching Round, only information that remains available after Attention and Working-Memory processing reaches the Knowledge Encoder and is transformed into an Explicit-LTM entry. Explicit LTM is therefore not a complete copy of the classroom record, but an explicit knowledge representation produced from the outcome of the preceding processing stages. The example in Figure~\ref{fig:persona-cpb-answer-information-source} illustrates this distinction: the Persona branch preserves the original Teaching Rounds and classroom interaction, whereas the CPB branch stores knowledge-oriented memory entries produced through processing and encoding.

Consequently, the two representations do not access the same type of information during assessment. A persona-prompted learner can answer from the complete accumulated teaching history. A CPB learner can access only information that entered Explicit LTM; instructional content made unavailable during an earlier Attention or Working-Memory stage does not reappear merely because the learner has entered the Assessment Phase. From a knowledge-representation perspective, the two approaches can therefore be characterised as \textbf{full-context-based answering} and \textbf{explicit-memory-based answering}. The former preserves continuing access to the full classroom history, whereas the latter connects teaching to assessment through an explicit memory state produced by the learning pipeline. This distinction describes how the two simulation architectures operationalise learner knowledge state; it does not presuppose that either approach is inherently more accurate or psychologically faithful.

The answer-stage prompt structures shown in Figure~\ref{fig:persona-cpb-answering-prompts} further reflect these different forms of learner representation. The persona-prompted learner receives an explicit ADHD-related \texttt{[COGNITIVE\_PROMPT]} in its system prompt. This component was adapted from the ADHD persona design of Gonnermann-Müller et al. \cite{gonnermann-muller-etal-2026-adhd-persona}. Its user message contains the accumulated \texttt{[CLASSROOM\_EXPERIENCE]}. The current \texttt{[ASSESSMENT\_QUESTION]} is added separately, so answer generation is conditioned jointly by the persona instructions and the complete teaching context.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_prompt_cpb_answering_prompt_comparison.pdf}
    \caption{Answer-stage prompt composition in Persona Prompting and CPB.}
    \label{fig:persona-cpb-answering-prompts}
\end{figure}

CPB does not provide an ADHD identity or ADHD-related role-playing instruction at the answer stage. Its system message instead contains a \texttt{[MEMORY\_RESTRICTION]} requiring the model to answer the current \texttt{[QUESTION]} using only the process-derived \texttt{[LEARNED\_MEMORY]}. The ADHD-related Attention and Working-Memory characteristics are thus represented primarily by learning-stage mechanisms rather than being restated as response-level instructions. Complete system-prompt examples for the two representations are provided in Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:appendix-prompt-adhd-example} and~\ref{fig:appendix-cpb-prompt-example}.

The \texttt{[LANGUAGE\_ABILITY]} and \texttt{[PERSONALITY\_DESCRIPTION]} components shown in Figure~\ref{fig:persona-cpb-answering-prompts} are enabled only in Study 3; they are absent from Studies 1 and 2. These components were adapted from \textit{Personality-aware Student Simulation for Conversational Intelligent Tutoring Systems} \cite{liu-etal-2024-personality}. They are added as response-stage attributes to both learner representations in order to examine how additional profile dimensions affect assessment behaviour and the previously established CPB patterns. Both branches otherwise share the core student instruction and receive the same assessment task.

The comparison can be summarised at three related levels: how learner characteristics are represented, what knowledge representation is retained after learning, and how the retained information participates in answer generation. \section{Cognitive-Process-Based Mechanisms}
\label{sec:cpb-mechanisms}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_cpb_learning_information_flow.pdf}
    \caption{CPB learning-phase information flow from the frozen Teaching Round to Explicit LTM.}
    \label{fig:cpb-learning-information-flow}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_cpb_assessment_phase_flow.pdf}
    \caption{CPB assessment-phase information flow under Explicit-LTM-constrained answering.}
    \label{fig:cpb-assessment-phase-flow}
\end{figure}

Throughout the CPB pipeline, \(r\) indexes the frozen Teaching Round and \(c\) indexes the experimental condition. \(S_r\) denotes the condition-invariant instructional Source; \(P_{r,c}\) denotes the post-Attention information state; \(A_{r,c}\) denotes the Available Input remaining after Working-Memory Processing; and \(L_{r,c}\) denotes the Explicit LTM generated by the shared Knowledge Encoder.

As illustrated in Figures~\ref{fig:cpb-learning-information-flow} and~\ref{fig:cpb-assessment-phase-flow}, CPB represents learning as a sequence of explicit information-state transformations. For each Teaching Round, the frozen instructional Source \(S_r\) first enters the Attention Filter, producing the post-Attention input \(P_{r,c}\). This information then enters Working-Memory Processing, where instructional processing demand and the condition-specific WM capacity further constrain it to form the Available Input \(A_{r,c}\). The Knowledge Encoder shared across all experimental conditions transforms this input into Explicit LTM \(L_{r,c}\). During the Assessment Phase, the CPB learner no longer accesses the original instructional materials; it answers the current assessment question using only the accumulated Explicit LTM. The CPB learning process can therefore be expressed as the continuous transformation \(S_r \rightarrow P_{r,c} \rightarrow A_{r,c} \rightarrow L_{r,c}\). Attention and Working Memory constitute the principal ADHD-related experimental constraints, whereas Knowledge Encoding remains a shared downstream stage.

\subsection{Attention Filter}
\label{subsec:attention-filter}

\subsubsection{Attention Filter Implementation}
\label{subsubsec:attention-filter-implementation}

As reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:adhd-learning-processes}, ADHD-related attentional differences can include increased susceptibility to task-irrelevant distraction, but the effects of distraction vary across learners and task conditions. The present study therefore models \textbf{susceptibility to controlled distraction} as a bounded functional abstraction rather than attempting to reproduce human attentional control as a whole. The implementation follows a simple principle: a distractor event is inserted immediately before a predefined assessment-relevant target sentence; when that distractor triggers the Attention Filter, the target sentence becomes unavailable for subsequent processing. Distraction is thereby represented through its downstream effect on information availability rather than through a persona-level instruction such as ``be easily distracted.''

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth,trim=720bp 80bp 720bp 80bp,clip,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_attention_filter_decision_flow.png}
    \caption{Attention Filter control flow.}
    \label{fig:attention-filter-decision-flow}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:attention-filter-decision-flow} summarises the control flow. For each Teaching Round, the mechanism first checks whether a frozen distractor assignment is present. If present, triggering is determined by the condition-specific Attention probability \(p^A_c\). A triggered distractor makes its predefined target sentence unavailable; otherwise, the instructional content is preserved. All branches produce the post-Attention state \(P_{r,c}\).

The affected instructional information is fixed before student simulation. Each Independent Question is already mapped to its supporting source sentence or sentences; one of these sentences is selected as the Attention target, and a bracketed distractor event is inserted immediately before it in the distracted version of the teaching material. Each assignment records \texttt{linked\_question\_id}, \texttt{distractor\_event}, and \texttt{target\_sentence\_id}. Integrative Questions do not receive additional independent distractor assignments. For example:

\begin{quote}
\footnotesize\ttfamily
Finance examines how money is obtained, managed, moved, and used.

[A seagull flies past and lands on the windowsill]

Finance helps individuals and organizations understand sources and uses of cash and make decisions when outcomes are uncertain.
\end{quote}

Here, the sentence following the bracketed distractor is the frozen target. The associated assessment question is not provided to the student model during learning, and the Attention mechanism does not dynamically select information based on the later question. The term \textit{question-targeted} therefore refers only to the preregistered alignment between the distractor, the affected source evidence, and the subsequent assessment item.

For an eligible distractor in Teaching Round \(r\), repeated run \(j\), and condition \(c\), the trigger decision is sampled as

\[
Z^A_{r,c,j}\sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p^A_c),
\]

where \(Z^A_{r,c,j}=1\) indicates that the distractor triggers the Attention Filter and its predefined target sentence becomes unavailable, while \(Z^A_{r,c,j}=0\) leaves the target sentence available. The boundary values \(p^A_c=0\) and \(p^A_c=1\) represent deterministic no-trigger and always-trigger conditions. Run-specific random seeds, draws, trigger decisions, and affected target identifiers are retained so that each stochastic Attention trajectory can be reconstructed.

The resulting transformation can be written as

\[
X_r \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Attention}} P_{r,c},
\]

where \(X_r\) is the presented classroom input, including any assigned distractor, and \(P_{r,c}\) is the instructional information that remains available for subsequent Working-Memory Processing. The Attention Filter therefore affects only downstream information availability: it does not directly modify Explicit LTM, generate assessment answers, or determine which information is relevant at answer time.

Treating a triggered target sentence as unavailable is a study-specific computational abstraction chosen for controllability and traceability, rather than a claim that human attentional disruption literally deletes information. The hard-deletion choice and its operationalisation sensitivity are examined in the next section, while study-specific values of \(p^A_c\) are defined in Sections~\ref{subsubsec:study1-conditions} and~\ref{subsubsec:study2-conditions}.

\subsubsection{Operationalisation Sensitivity Check}
\label{sec:attention-operationalisation-sensitivity}

Direct deletion provides a simple and controllable way to represent instructional information as unavailable, and similar input-removal operations have been used in NLP perturbation studies \cite{feng-etal-2018-pathologies}. However, prior work also shows that different representations of missing information, such as deletion and masking, can produce different downstream model behaviour \cite{lewis-etal-2020-bart}. Therefore, the choice of unavailable-content representation cannot be assumed to be behaviourally neutral.

To test whether the observed CPB effects depend on this implementation choice, the present study compares two representation policies: \textbf{hard deletion}, which removes the target sentence entirely, and \textbf{explicit masking}, which removes its original semantic content while retaining a visible missingness cue. This sensitivity check is used only to assess whether the main Attention-related results are robust to the surface representation of unavailable information.

\[
g_{\mathrm{delete}}(s)=\emptyset,
\]

\[
g_{\mathrm{mask}}(s)
=
\texttt{[CONTENT UNAVAILABLE: ...]}.
\]

The two implementations therefore have different informational implications. Hard deletion prevents the target instructional content from entering subsequent processing, whereas explicit masking removes the original semantics but additionally provides the model with a metacue indicating that information is missing at that location. Character-length matching guarantees only the same Python character count and does not imply an equivalent token count.

To test whether this representation choice affects downstream processing, the study conducted an independent sensitivity experiment using two frozen lessons, L01 and L02. The experiment included three mechanism conditions in which information could become unavailable:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Attention ON / WM OFF;
    \item Attention OFF / WM ON; and
    \item Attention ON / WM ON.
\end{enumerate}

In this experiment, Attention ON means that the Attention Filter is triggered with 100\% probability. Attention OFF / WM OFF was excluded because this condition contains no sentence marked as unavailable and therefore produces no actual difference between the two representation policies.

Each mechanism condition was run three times under each of the two representation policies, hard deletion and explicit masking. Each lesson contained seven frozen assessment questions, including six Independent Questions and one cross-round Integrative Question, producing 252 answers in total. Across all paired-round checks, the Attention trigger, Attention-unavailable sentence identifiers, and the other recorded CPB mechanism variables were identical between policies in 100\% of cases. The primary comparison was defined as

\[
\Delta \mathrm{Score}
=
\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{mask}}
-
\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{delete}}.
\]

To determine whether masking could be treated as a practically interchangeable implementation of deletion, a practical-equivalence margin of \(\pm 0.5\) points was specified in advance. A cluster bootstrap over the 14 frozen question clusters was used to calculate a 95\% interval. Practical equivalence was considered supported only if

\[
\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}
\subseteq
[-0.5,+0.5].
\]

The sensitivity analysis showed that most matched outputs were unchanged across the two implementations, but practical equivalence was not established within the prespecified margin. The overall mask-minus-deletion score difference was

\[
\Delta=-0.370,
\]

with a 95\% interval of

\[
[-1.005,0.106].
\]

Although the interval crossed zero and therefore did not support a stable directional difference, it was not fully contained within the \(\pm 0.5\) practical-equivalence margin. At the same time, 91.27\% of paired question scores were identical, while the small number of discrepant cases produced comparatively large downstream score shifts and were concentrated mainly in conditions involving the Attention manipulation.

Hard deletion was ultimately selected not because the sensitivity experiment demonstrated that it was psychologically closer to human ADHD-related attention than masking, but because of the combined considerations of \textit{construct correspondence}, \textit{minimal intervention}, and the sensitivity evidence. The Attention Filter is intended to represent a state in which target instructional information is no longer available to downstream processing. Hard deletion implements this change directly, whereas explicit masking removes the target semantics while introducing an additional explicit missingness cue. Hard deletion was therefore frozen as the formal unavailable-content policy.

This choice does not imply that hard deletion has been validated as the psychological mechanism underlying human ADHD-related attentional disruption. Direct deletion reduces both target semantics and the length of the downstream instructional input and therefore remains a study-specific computational operationalisation. The present sensitivity evidence supports it only as the more direct and traceable formal implementation used in this study, with fewer additional representational signals.

\subsection{Working-Memory Processing Capacity}

\subsubsection{Working-Memory Mechanism Implementation}
\label{sec:wm-mechanism-implementation}

As reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:adhd-learning-processes}, Working Memory (WM) is a limited processing system involved in maintaining and manipulating task-relevant information, and ADHD-related WM differences may become more consequential as processing demands increase. The present study therefore models \textbf{sensitivity to instructional processing demand} as a bounded functional abstraction rather than attempting to reproduce human Working Memory as a whole. Within CPB, the WM mechanism operates after Attention: when the frozen processing demand of a Teaching Round exceeds the capacity threshold assigned to the current condition, the earliest instructional sentence still available after Attention is made unavailable before Knowledge Encoding.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_working_memory_mechanism_flow.png}
    \caption{Working-Memory mechanism flow. Frozen source-round demand $D(R_r)$ is compared with the condition-specific threshold $C^{WM}_c$. When demand exceeds capacity, the earliest sentence remaining in $P_{r,c}$ is removed before the resulting Available Input $A_{r,c}$ proceeds to Knowledge Encoding.}
    \label{fig:working-memory-mechanism-flow}
\end{figure}

For Teaching Round $R_r$, the WM trigger is determined by

\[
\operatorname{WMOverflow}(R_r,c)
=
\mathbf{1}\left[D(R_r)>C^{WM}_c\right],
\]

where $D(R_r)$ is the Processing Demand Bits (PDB) value calculated and frozen for the original source round, and $C^{WM}_c$ is the capacity threshold assigned to condition $c$. The same $D(R_r)$ is used across all learner conditions and is not recalculated after Attention, ensuring that instructional demand remains a condition-independent property of the source material. Unlike the probabilistic Attention trigger, the WM rule is deterministic: for a fixed Teaching Round and capacity threshold, the trigger decision is always the same.

When overflow occurs, the mechanism acts only on instructional information that remains available in the post-Attention state $P_{r,c}$. If at least one sentence remains, the earliest available sentence in the original teaching order is selected for removal:

\[
R^{WM}_{r,c}
=
\{\operatorname{First}(P_{r,c})\},
\]

and the Available Input passed to Knowledge Encoding becomes

\[
A_{r,c}
=
P_{r,c}\setminus R^{WM}_{r,c}.
\]

If WM does not trigger, $A_{r,c}=P_{r,c}$. Each Teaching Round permits at most one WM-related sentence removal, and information already made unavailable by Attention cannot be removed again. The mechanism is therefore implemented as a \textbf{deterministic within-round FIFO-style information-restriction rule}.

The overall WM transformation can thus be represented as

\[
P_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Working\ Memory}}
A_{r,c},
\]

where Attention determines what information remains available before WM processing, and WM further constrains how much of that remaining information can proceed to the shared Knowledge Encoder under the current demand--capacity condition. The capacity threshold $C^{WM}_c$ uses the same numerical scale as PDB but does not represent a literal number of human memory slots, sentences, or tokens. It is a study-specific control parameter for manipulating processing-constraint strength. The WM mechanism does not attempt to estimate the physiological working-memory capacity of real students. Study-specific thresholds and their experimental roles are reported in Sections~\ref{subsubsec:study1-conditions} and~\ref{subsubsec:study2-conditions}.

\subsection{Knowledge Encoding}

\subsubsection{Knowledge Encoding Implementation}
\label{sec:knowledge-encoding-implementation}

As reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:process-state-learner-modelling}, information that remains available after Attention and Working-Memory processing is conceptually distinct from a persistent learner knowledge state. CPB therefore includes a separate Knowledge-Encoding stage between Available Input and Explicit LTM. Encoding is not manipulated as an additional ADHD-specific mechanism; instead, all learner conditions use the same Encoder procedure so that differences in downstream knowledge state can be interpreted relative to the information that actually reaches Encoding. For Teaching Round $r$ and condition $c$, the transformation is represented as

\[
A_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Encoding}}
L_{r,c},
\]

where $A_{r,c}$ is the Available Input remaining after Attention and Working-Memory processing and $L_{r,c}$ is the resulting Explicit LTM entry.

Within CPB, Knowledge Encoding uses each Teaching Round as its basic unit of processing and is executed after Attention and Working-Memory processing. For Teaching Round $r$ under condition $c$, the Encoder receives only the Available Input $A_{r,c}$ produced by the preceding mechanisms, rather than the original instructional source or the complete Teaching Round. Information already marked as unavailable by Attention or WM is not supplied to the Encoder again. The process is represented as

\[
L_{r,c}
=
\operatorname{Encode}(A_{r,c}),
\]

where $L_{r,c}$ is the round-level Explicit LTM entry formed after Encoding. The preceding mechanisms therefore determine \textbf{what information reaches Encoding}, while the Knowledge Encoder performs a representational transformation only on the information that actually reaches this stage.

Figure~\ref{fig:knowledge-encoding-prompt} presents the frozen Knowledge-Encoding prompt used in the formal experiments. The prompt requires the model to generate one brief long-term memory entry based only on the classroom information actually received, preserve the main concepts and their relationships in its own words, and avoid using outside knowledge or completing information absent from the input. Output is restricted to a single plain-text paragraph to reduce additional formatting differences in the subsequent Explicit LTM representation.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_knowledge_encoding_prompt_example.pdf}
    \caption{Frozen prompt template used by the shared Knowledge Encoder.}
    \label{fig:knowledge-encoding-prompt}
\end{figure}

Knowledge Encoding is not intended to copy the Available Input verbatim, but to transform it into a concise semantic knowledge representation. Accordingly, $A_{r,c}$ and $L_{r,c}$ are not assumed to be identical. The Encoder may paraphrase, compress, and reorganise the input, but the resulting memory entry should remain constrained by the information provided in $A_{r,c}$ and should not recover instructional content that has already become unavailable. Figure~\ref{fig:knowledge-encoding-example} presents an actual Encoding example: the Available Instructional Input $A_{r,c}$ entering the Encoder appears on the left, and the Encoded LTM Entry $L_{r,c}$ generated from the same content appears on the right. The example shows that the main concepts and their relationships can be retained after Encoding even though the wording and degree of information compression differ from the input text.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Figures/Chapter3/export/ch03_knowledge_encoding_example.pdf}
    \caption{Example transformation from Available Instructional Input to an encoded LTM entry.}
    \label{fig:knowledge-encoding-example}
\end{figure}

The Encoding procedure is executed independently for each Teaching Round and produces at most one corresponding $L_{r,c}$. When no instructional information remains available for Encoding after Attention and WM processing, such that $A_{r,c}=\emptyset$, the system does not independently generate a new instructional memory from distractor content, prompt context, or the model's pretrained knowledge. This rule ensures that Explicit LTM originates only from instructional information that remains available after the preceding cognitive-processing stages and preserves a traceable relationship between Available Input and the encoded knowledge state.

Knowledge Encoding is not treated as an ADHD-specific mechanism in this study; instead, a fixed, shared Knowledge-Encoding procedure is used. All conditions use the same Encoder model, encoding prompt, input structure, and generation configuration. Attention, WM-constraint level, and other learner-condition metadata do not alter the Encoding instruction. This modelling decision does not constitute an empirical assumption that real ADHD and NT learners have completely identical encoding abilities.

\subsubsection{Pretrained-Knowledge and Memory-Constrained Answering}
\label{sec:pretrained-knowledge-memory-control}

In the formal experiments, the CPB Assessment Phase requires the simulated learner to answer questions using only the Explicit LTM formed during the Teaching Phase. However, because the large language model already contains pretrained knowledge, two alternative explanations must first be excluded. First, the student model may be able to answer most assessment questions using pretrained knowledge without any exposure to the course material, thereby compressing the observable space for the subsequent learning manipulations. Second, even when Explicit LTM is provided and the prompt requires the model to answer only from memory, the model may bypass this restriction and use its existing financial knowledge to complete the answer. To test these two prerequisites, the study conducted a \textbf{Question-Only Baseline} and a \textbf{Biology-Memory-Only Control}.

In the Question-Only Baseline, DeepSeek V4-Flash received no course material, teaching history, or Explicit LTM and directly answered the 49 financial questions in the formal assessment set. The generated answers were then scored by the same Qwen3.7-Max Judge used in the formal experiments. The mean score in this condition was

\[
\bar S_{\mathrm{Baseline}}=7.823.
\]

This result shows that DeepSeek V4-Flash already possesses some pretrained financial-domain knowledge, so the formal experiments cannot assume that assessment performance originates entirely from the Teaching Phase. At the same time, the baseline remained below the maximum score of 10, indicating that the question set retained observable learning headroom.

The Biology memory covered cells, genetics, physiology, evolution, and ecology and contained no finance, accounting, market, or investment knowledge. DeepSeek V4-Flash then answered the same 49 financial assessment questions under the same memory-constrained answering prompt.

The answering prompt explicitly required the model to answer only from the provided learned memory and prohibited the use of information not contained in that memory. All answers were evaluated by the same Qwen3.7-Max Judge against the same 137 frozen atomic criteria.

In the Biology-Memory-Only condition, all 49 assessment questions received a final score of zero. The Judge labelled all 137 rubric criteria as \texttt{Absent}, with \texttt{Correct}=0 and \texttt{Contradicted}=0, and the mean score was zero for every one of the seven lessons. This result supports the \textbf{operational effectiveness of memory-access restriction in the current student-model setting}. More precisely, when the provided Explicit LTM lacked the information required to answer an assessment question, DeepSeek V4-Flash did not exhibit behaviour indicating that it used pretrained financial knowledge to complete the correct answer under the current answering prompt. The control therefore verifies that the model's compliance with the instruction to answer only from the supplied memory operated as expected.

On the basis of these two controls, the subsequent formal assessment procedure treats the frozen Explicit Knowledge State as the simulated learner's only experimental knowledge source. For condition $c$, the round-level LTM entries formed across Teaching Rounds are accumulated and frozen as

\[
\mathcal{L}_c=
\{L_{1,c},L_{2,c},\ldots,L_{R,c}\}.
\]

Each assessment question $Q_q$ independently generates an answer from the same frozen Explicit Knowledge State:

\[
\mathcal{L}_c + Q_q
\rightarrow
Y_{q,c}.
\]

The original instructional source, complete teaching history, and information previously marked as unavailable by Attention or Working Memory are not supplied to the answering model. Knowledge Encoding and question answering represent, respectively, the same simulated learner's knowledge-formation and knowledge-use stages. Because the corresponding encoding and memory-restriction prompts behaved stably with DeepSeek V4-Flash, the formal experiments use DeepSeek V4-Flash for both stages to avoid introducing additional cross-model differences.

Taken together, the Question-Only Baseline shows that the student model had not completely mastered the assessment set without teaching and that observable learning headroom remained. The Biology-Memory-Only Control shows that answer generation was effectively constrained by the supplied Explicit LTM under the current prompt and model configuration. These two controls jointly strengthen the attribution of subsequent performance differences to the manipulated learning processes and the Explicit Knowledge States they produced, rather than to unrestricted use of pretrained financial knowledge during the Assessment Phase.


\section{Research Design and Evaluation Strategy}

\subsection{Study 1---Mechanistic Validity of CPB}
\label{subsec:study1-design}

\subsubsection{Research Question and Subquestions}

Study 1 addresses RQ1 by examining whether the CPB processing pipeline produces correct, attributable, and traceable information-state changes according to the predefined rules. RQ1 is:

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{RQ1---Mechanism:} Does the CPB processing pipeline execute its intended rules and produce traceable transitions from instructional information, through attention, working-memory processing and encoding, to Explicit LTM?
\end{quote}

To answer this question, Study 1 divides RQ1 into two subquestions. SQ1 examines whether the Attention and Working-Memory mechanisms execute their registered rules correctly. SQ2 then examines whether these mechanisms produce attributable information-state changes at the intended processing stages and whether those changes propagate through the pipeline to the final Explicit LTM.

\paragraph{SQ1---Mechanism Execution.}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Do the Attention and Working-Memory mechanisms execute their registered rules correctly?}
\end{quote}

SQ1 examines whether Attention triggering and target removal follow the predefined rules while preserving non-target instructional information, and whether WM overflow and FIFO-style removal occur only when the registered capacity constraint is exceeded.

\paragraph{SQ2---Traceable Information-State Transitions and Propagation.}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Do the implemented mechanisms produce intended and attributable information-state changes, and do these changes propagate through the processing pipeline to Explicit LTM?}
\end{quote}

SQ2 examines whether the CPB information flow

\[
S_r
\xrightarrow{\text{Attention Filter}}
P_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\text{Working-Memory Processing}}
A_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\text{Shared Knowledge Encoder}}
L_{r,c}.
\]

shows the intended stage-specific transitions. The analysis first identifies the local changes introduced by Attention, Working-Memory Processing, and Encoding, and then examines how Attention- and WM-related removals jointly produce the Available Input state and whether the resulting information differences remain observable after encoding in Explicit LTM.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:study1-validation-logic}, SQ1 validates the execution of the registered Attention and Working-Memory rules, whereas SQ2 traces stage-specific information-state transitions and their cumulative propagation to Explicit LTM. The corresponding evaluation measures are defined in Section~\ref{subsubsec:study1-measures}.

\subsubsection{Experimental Design and Conditions}
\label{subsubsec:study1-conditions}

Study 1 uses a $2 \times 2$ factorial ablation design that independently manipulates the OFF/ON states of the Attention Filter and Working-Memory Processing Capacity:

\[
\text{Attention OFF/ON}
\times
\text{WM OFF/ON}.
\]

This design produces four experimental conditions, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:study1-conditions}. A1W0 and A0W1 isolate the independent effects of Attention and WM, respectively; A1W1 examines their cumulative operation when enabled sequentially in the CPB processing order; and A0W0 provides a reference condition without Attention- or WM-related information restriction. The purpose is not to construct levels of ADHD severity, but to maximise \textit{mechanism identifiability} through explicit boundary conditions.

Study 1 implements these OFF/ON manipulations using deterministic boundary parameters. Under the mechanism rules defined in Sections~3.4.1 and~3.4.2, Attention ON uses $p^A_c=1$, causing every eligible distractor to trigger the Attention Filter so that target localisation and removal can be tested directly. Attention OFF uses $p^A_c=0$ and produces no Attention-attributed removal. WM ON uses the median boundary of the frozen PDB distribution, $C^{WM}_c=321.9937895$ bits; within the calibration set comprising the source rounds associated with the 42 Independent Questions, 21 source rounds exceed this threshold. The 21/42 value describes threshold calibration, whereas the actual number of WM triggers across the complete Teaching Phase is a mechanism-execution result for SQ1. WM OFF uses $10^{10}$ bits as an effectively non-binding implementation sentinel and disables WM removal. Use of the median boundary enables Study 1 to validate deterministically the same WM rule used by the Study 2 Medium condition. Study 1 itself terminates at Explicit LTM and does not analyse answer performance. These parameters establish mechanism-validation boundary conditions and do not represent ADHD severity or the attention and working-memory capacity of real individuals.

Under A1W1, the two mechanisms execute sequentially in the registered CPB order:

\[
S_r
\xrightarrow{\text{Attention Filter}}
P_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\text{Working-Memory Processing}}
A_{r,c}.
\]

Attention first determines which instructional sentences remain available after distraction. WM can then restrict only this remaining information. Its trigger decision still uses the Teaching Round's frozen $D(R_r)$; PDB is not recalculated after Attention removal. A1W1 therefore represents the cumulative operation of two registered mechanisms in a fixed order rather than a separate joint mechanism.

Except for the Attention and WM states, all learning-stage components are held constant across the four conditions. Every condition uses the same frozen Teaching Rounds, distractor assignments, student-model configuration, and Knowledge Encoder. In particular, although A0W0 disables the Attention and WM constraints, it still undergoes normal Knowledge Encoding and is therefore defined as an \textit{encoding-only reference}, rather than a perfect-memory condition.

Through this $2\times2$ design, Study 1 can observe separately the information-state differences produced by Attention, WM, and their sequential combination while holding the instructional materials and Encoding configuration constant.

Study 1 uses seven frozen instructional materials, with each of the four $2\times2$ conditions executed once as a canonical learning-stage run. Each condition retains mechanism-execution logs, stage-specific sentence identifiers and word counts, Available Input, and the final Explicit LTM. Study 1 terminates at this knowledge state and does not generate assessment answers or conduct Judge scoring.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Measures}
\label{subsubsec:study1-measures}

To answer SQ1 and SQ2, Study 1 uses two connected validation domains. SQ1 uses implementation-fidelity measures to check whether Attention and Working Memory trigger and execute according to the preregistered rules. SQ2 then combines stage-specific transition measures with cumulative-propagation measures to test whether the resulting information-state changes can be located and attributed, and whether they are reflected in the embedding-based semantic correspondence between the final Explicit LTM and the Source.

Text-quantity availability measures use deterministic $\operatorname{WordCount}(X)$ calculations that count instructional content only and exclude distractor labels, control markers, and other experimental prompt text. The counts describe how much instructional information remains available before and after different processing stages; they are not interpreted as real human attention levels, working-memory capacities, or percentages of knowledge retention.

\paragraph{SQ1---Mechanism Execution.}

SQ1 first checks whether the manipulated mechanisms implement the rules defined in Section~3.4. In Study 1, Attention ON uses $p_A=1$, so every Teaching Round with an eligible distractor assignment should trigger the Attention Filter and make its mapped target sentence unavailable. Attention OFF uses $p_A=0$ and should produce no Attention-attributed removal. WM follows the deterministic capacity rule $D(R_r)>C^{WM}_c$ and, when triggered, removes the earliest instructional sentence that remains available after Attention. The SQ1 measures therefore test both whether each mechanism triggers when it should and whether it applies the correct information-selection rule after triggering.

Attention execution is assessed using two complementary measures. \textbf{Attention Target-Removal Fidelity (ATRF)} quantifies the proportion of expected Attention targets that are actually removed:

\[
ATRF_c=
\frac{
\sum_r
\left|
T^{\mathrm{expected}}_{r,c}
\cap
T^{\mathrm{removed}}_{r,c}
\right|
}{
\sum_r
\left|
T^{\mathrm{expected}}_{r,c}
\right|
}.
\]

Here, $T^{\mathrm{expected}}_{r,c}$ denotes the sentence identifiers that should be removed under the frozen distractor mapping and trigger setting for condition $c$, and $T^{\mathrm{removed}}_{r,c}$ denotes the identifiers actually removed in the execution log. Values closer to 1 indicate more complete execution of the intended target removal; $ATRF_c=1$ means that all expected targets were correctly removed. Because Attention OFF has no expected target removals, ATRF applies to rounds and conditions in which removal is expected.

The complementary \textbf{Non-Target Preservation Rate (NTPR)} checks whether information that should remain available after Attention is preserved. Let $N^{\mathrm{expected}}_{r,c}$ denote instructional sentences expected to remain available and $N^{\mathrm{preserved}}_{r,c}$ the sentences actually preserved. Then:

\[
NTPR_c=
\frac{
\sum_r
\left|
N^{\mathrm{expected}}_{r,c}
\cap
N^{\mathrm{preserved}}_{r,c}
\right|
}{
\sum_r
\left|
N^{\mathrm{expected}}_{r,c}
\right|
}.
\]

Values closer to 1 indicate fewer unintended Attention removals; $NTPR_c=1$ means that all non-target information was correctly preserved. ATRF and NTPR therefore assess correct removal and correct preservation separately.

WM execution is also assessed using two complementary measures. \textbf{Working-Memory Trigger Agreement (WMTA)} quantifies whether the trigger status in the execution log agrees with the registered capacity rule:

\[
WMTA_c
=
\frac{1}{R}
\sum_r
\mathbf{1}
\left[
z^{WM}_{r,c}
=
\mathbf{1}
\left[
D(R_r)>C^{WM}_c
\right]
\right],
\]

where $z^{WM}_{r,c}$ is the recorded WM trigger status. Values closer to 1 indicate stronger agreement between observed triggering and the threshold rule; $WMTA_c=1$ means that every Teaching Round triggered exactly when required.

For rounds in which WM triggers and the Post-Attention Input still contains removable instructional information, \textbf{FIFO Rule Fidelity (FRF)} is calculated. Let $\mathcal E_c$ denote this set of eligible rounds:

\[
FRF_c=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal E_c|}
\sum_{r\in\mathcal E_c}
\mathbf{1}
\left[
R^{WM}_{r,c}
=
\{\operatorname{First}(P_{r,c})\}
\right].
\]

Values closer to 1 indicate more consistent execution of the predefined within-round FIFO-style information-restriction rule; $FRF_c=1$ means that the earliest available instructional sentence was correctly removed in every eligible overflow round. ATRF, NTPR, WMTA, and FRF are implementation-fidelity measures defined from the frozen mechanism rules and execution logs. They verify computational execution rather than human ADHD cognitive fidelity.
\paragraph{SQ2---Traceable Information-State Transitions and Propagation.}

\textbf{Stage-specific transition measures.}

After mechanism execution has been established, SQ2 evaluates information-state changes at the transitions:

\[
S_r
\xrightarrow{\text{Attention}}
P_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\text{WM}}
A_{r,c}
\xrightarrow{\text{Encoding}}
L_{r,c}.
\]

The Attention stage uses the \textbf{Attention-stage Availability Ratio (AAR)} to quantify the proportion of Source information that remains available after the Attention Filter. For lesson $l$ and condition $c$:

\[
AAR_{l,c}
=
\frac{
\sum_{r\in l}\operatorname{WordCount}(P_{r,c})
}{
\sum_{r\in l}\operatorname{WordCount}(S_r)
}.
\]

$AAR_{l,c}=1$ means that no instructional information in the lesson became unavailable at the Attention stage; lower values indicate greater Attention-attributed loss. AAR is an information-availability ratio computed from the explicit processing trace, not an ``attention percentage''.

The WM stage uses the \textbf{Working-Memory-stage Availability Ratio (WMAR)} to quantify the proportion of Post-Attention Input that passes the WM constraint and reaches the Knowledge Encoder:

\[
WMAR_{l,c}
=
\frac{
\sum_{r\in l}\operatorname{WordCount}(A_{r,c})
}{
\sum_{r\in l}\operatorname{WordCount}(P_{r,c})
}.
\]

Using $P_{r,c}$ rather than the complete Source as the denominator separates WM-attributed restriction from preceding Attention loss. $WMAR_{l,c}=1$ indicates that WM caused no additional reduction in the Post-Attention Input; lower values indicate greater restriction among the information that passed Attention. AAR and WMAR therefore localise the information-availability effects of Attention and WM, respectively.

Knowledge Encoding is shared across conditions and is not an additional ADHD-specific manipulation. This stage is evaluated through \textbf{Encoding Semantic Recall (ESR)}, a diagnostic of semantic correspondence between the Available Input and the generated Explicit LTM:

\[
ESR_{l,c}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal R^+_{l,c}|}
\sum_{r\in\mathcal R^+_{l,c}}
B_R(A_{r,c},L_{r,c}),
\]

where:

\[
\mathcal R^+_{l,c}
=
\left\{
r\in l:
\operatorname{WordCount}(A_{r,c})>0
\right\}.
\]

$B_R(A_{r,c},L_{r,c})$ is BERTScore Recall calculated with Available Input as the reference and Explicit LTM as the candidate. BERTScore uses contextual embeddings to match candidate and reference tokens and can therefore identify paraphrases and semantically similar expressions that exact lexical-overlap measures may miss \cite{zhang2019bertscore}. The frozen configuration uses the \path{microsoft/deberta-xlarge-mnli} model, with Recall as the reported component, inverse-document-frequency weighting disabled, and baseline rescaling enabled.

Recall is used because the CPB Encoder is instructed to compress and paraphrase the Available Input into a short LTM entry. The relevant question is how much of the semantic content reaching the Encoder is represented in the generated LTM, not whether input and output are lexically identical or equally long. Higher ESR indicates stronger semantic coverage of the received Available Input; lower ESR indicates that more input semantics lack a corresponding representation in the LTM. Because baseline rescaling is applied, ESR is not interpreted as a strict probability on a 0--1 retention scale. Rounds with an empty $A_{r,c}$ are excluded because they contain no instructional content for the Encoder to transform.
\textbf{Cumulative-propagation measures.}

The cumulative-propagation component of SQ2 moves from local transitions to the complete learning pipeline and examines whether Attention- and WM-attributed information changes accumulate in the Available Input reaching the Encoder and in the final Explicit LTM.

The \textbf{Source-Word Availability Ratio (SWAR)} quantifies the proportion of the original Source that remains available to the Encoder after both Attention and WM:

\[
SWAR_{l,c}
=
\frac{
\sum_{r\in l}\operatorname{WordCount}(A_{r,c})
}{
\sum_{r\in l}\operatorname{WordCount}(S_r)
}.
\]

Values closer to 1 indicate that a larger proportion of the Source reaches the shared Knowledge Encoder; lower values indicate stronger cumulative upstream restriction. Under the same lesson-level aggregation:

\[
SWAR_{l,c}
=
AAR_{l,c}
\times
WMAR_{l,c}.
\]

SWAR is therefore not a separate cognitive mechanism measure, but the cumulative information-availability outcome of the Attention and WM stages.

As a supplementary decomposition, the frozen Source-word total in each condition is partitioned into Available Input, Attention-attributed loss, and WM-attributed loss:

\[
W_{\text{source},c}
=
W_{\text{available},c}
+
W_{\text{attention-loss},c}
+
W_{\text{WM-loss},c}.
\]

$W_{\text{available},c}$ is the total word count of $A_{r,c}$ across Teaching Rounds, and $W_{\text{attention-loss},c}$ and $W_{\text{WM-loss},c}$ are the cumulative word losses attributed to the two mechanisms. This decomposition supplements SWAR by showing the absolute composition and attribution of upstream information restriction.

\textbf{End-to-End Semantic Recall (E2ESR)} quantifies semantic correspondence between the complete instructional Source and the final Explicit LTM:

\[
E2ESR_{l,c}
=
\frac{1}{R_l}
\sum_{r\in l}
B_R(S_r,L_{r,c}),
\]

where $R_l$ is the number of Teaching Rounds in lesson $l$. The same frozen BERTScore Recall configuration is used \cite{zhang2019bertscore}, but ESR and E2ESR differ in their comparison objects and interpretations. ESR compares

\[
A_{r,c}\rightarrow L_{r,c},
\]

and diagnoses how the Encoder processes the information it actually receives. E2ESR instead compares

\[
S_r\rightarrow L_{r,c},
\]

and describes the overall contextual-embedding correspondence between the Source and Explicit LTM after Attention, WM, and Encoding. If a Teaching Round produces no LTM entry, its E2ESR contribution is set to zero under the frozen rule so that complete upstream information loss is represented in the cumulative semantic-correspondence measure. Higher E2ESR indicates stronger embedding-based semantic correspondence between the final Explicit LTM and the original instructional content; lower values indicate weaker overall correspondence.

E2ESR does not perform proposition extraction, entailment checking, or criterion-level fact matching. A high E2ESR therefore cannot demonstrate that every proposition, negation state, relation direction, causal structure, or qualifier in the Source has been retained in Explicit LTM. Conversely, a low E2ESR indicates only reduced overall embedding correspondence and does not identify which propositions are missing. Because the Recall component is used, the measure primarily checks whether Source content has an embedding match in LTM and does not directly identify unsupported additions in LTM. It is neither a percentage of human knowledge retention nor a proposition-level knowledge-retention measure; it is used only to diagnose changes in textual semantic correspondence across the complete learning pipeline. Here, ``end-to-end'' refers only to the Study 1 learning pipeline from Source to Explicit LTM and does not include answer generation or Judge scoring.

AAR, WMAR, ESR, SWAR, and E2ESR are first calculated at lesson level. The overall estimate for each condition is the unweighted mean across the seven frozen instructional materials, giving every lesson equal weight. A 95\% confidence interval is calculated by non-parametric bootstrap with lesson as the resampling unit: seven lessons are sampled with replacement, the unweighted mean is recalculated, and the procedure is repeated 10,000 times. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution define the interval. A fixed random seed is used so that the analysis is reproducible under the frozen inputs and analysis environment. These intervals describe descriptive robustness to the composition of the seven frozen lessons and do not represent uncertainty in the human ADHD population.

\subsection{Study 2---Comparative Representational Validity}
\label{subsec:study2-design}

\subsubsection{Research Question and Subquestions}
\label{subsubsec:study2-rq}

Study 2 addresses RQ2 by comparing whether persona prompting and CPB exhibit different and theoretically consistent behavioural sensitivity to controlled distraction and instructional processing demand. RQ2 is:

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{RQ2---Representation:} Compared with persona prompting, does CPB produce learning behaviour that shows more theoretically consistent sensitivity to controlled distraction and instructional processing demand?
\end{quote}

To answer this question, Study 2 divides RQ2 into two progressive subquestions. SQ1 examines only whether persona prompting and CPB produce systematic differentiation in assessment performance within their respective representations. SQ2 then examines whether the two representations exhibit process sensitivity in the directions predicted by the targeted ADHD-related Attention and Working-Memory hypotheses.

\paragraph{SQ1---Within-Representation Performance Differentiation.}

\begin{quote}
\small
\raggedright
\textbf{Under distracted materials, do the manipulated learner conditions produce systematic within-representation differences in assessment performance under Prompt-based and CPB-based simulation?}
\end{quote}

SQ1 contains two parallel, representation-internal branches under the common distracted-material condition. The Prompt branch examines whether Prompt-NT, Moderate-Intensity Prompt-ADHD, and High-Intensity Prompt-ADHD form a performance ordering corresponding to persona intensity. The CPB branch examines whether CPB Zero, Low, Medium, and High form a graded performance response as registered constraint severity increases. This subquestion asks only whether scores, run-to-run variability, and response length change systematically with each manipulation. It does not interpret a lower absolute score as inherently more consistent with ADHD cognitive mechanisms, and it does not compare the absolute Prompt and CPB scores to make a cross-representation judgement. Clean-material results are retained for the material-condition comparison and primary processing-demand analysis in SQ2, rather than treated as independent differentiation evidence for SQ1.

\paragraph{SQ2---ADHD-Theory-Consistent Process Sensitivity.}

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Which representation more consistently exhibits performance sensitivity to controlled distraction and instructional processing demand in the directions predicted by the targeted ADHD-related mechanisms?}
\end{quote}

SQ2 contains two complementary process-sensitive components. The controlled-distraction component compares performance change between clean and distracted materials within the same learner condition and interprets that change relative to the Prompt-NT or CPB Zero reference condition. The processing-demand component examines whether question performance decreases as source-round Processing Demand Bits (PDB) increase.

Overall, Study 2 distinguishes representation-internal performance differentiation from task-specific, ADHD-theory-consistent process sensitivity. The following section describes the experimental design and conditions used for these comparisons.

\subsubsection{Experimental Design and Conditions}
\label{subsubsec:study2-conditions}

Study 2 used a fully crossed learner-condition $\times$ material-condition design. The learner factor comprised three persona-prompted and four CPB learners. Each learner condition received both clean and distracted materials, with five independent runs in every learner--material cell.

Each instructional material contains six Independent Questions and one cross-round Integrative Question. Across the seven materials, the assessment therefore contains 42 Independent Questions and seven Integrative Questions. Each Independent Question maps to one unique source Teaching Round, and these 42 assessment-relevant source rounds form the calibration set used for the WM thresholds described below.

The persona-prompted branch comprised Prompt-NT and two Prompt-ADHD intensity conditions. The Prompt-ADHD descriptions used the text-based educational prompts provided by Gonnermann-Müller et al. \cite{gonnermann-muller-etal-2026-adhd-persona}. They retained the same ADHD-related behavioural content while varying intensity through frequency terms. The Moderate-Intensity condition primarily used terms such as \textit{sometimes}, \textit{occasionally}, and \textit{somewhat}; the High-Intensity condition primarily used terms such as \textit{often}, \textit{frequently}, and \textit{regularly}. Prompt-NT contained no ADHD-specific cognitive description and served as the persona-prompted reference condition.

The CPB branch comprised Zero, Low, Medium, and High. CPB Zero retained Teaching-Round processing, shared Knowledge Encoding, Explicit LTM accumulation, and memory-constrained answering while disabling both information-loss mechanisms. It therefore served as the \textit{zero-constraint CPB reference}. It produced no Attention- or WM-attributed information loss but retained the complete CPB architecture. Low, Medium, and High jointly varied Attention-trigger probability and WM-capacity threshold to impose progressively stronger processing constraints. Figure~\ref{fig:study2-wm-calibration} and Table~\ref{tab:study2-conditions} show, respectively, the basis for selecting the WM thresholds and the registered parameters for all learner conditions.

For CPB Zero, $10^{10}$ bits is an effectively non-binding implementation sentinel used while WM removal is disabled, rather than a meaningful capacity estimate. For the constrained conditions, increasing registered constraint severity combines a higher Attention-trigger probability with a lower WM capacity:

\[

>
C^{WM}_{\mathrm{Medium}}
>

\]

CPB Low, Medium, and High are therefore registered experimental constraint levels jointly constituted by the Attention and WM parameters, rather than clinically calibrated ADHD severity categories. Because both parameters change with severity, Study 2 tests their combined behavioural consequences and does not estimate the independent causal contribution of Attention or WM.

Both material conditions were derived from the same seven frozen instructional materials. The clean condition used instructional text without distractor events, whereas the distracted condition used instructional text containing frozen distractor events at the predefined mapped locations. Both simulated-student representations used DeepSeek V4 Flash as the student model and shared the same frozen instructional materials, assessment questions, and scoring framework. Their Assessment Phases followed their respective representation architectures: Prompt learners answered from the frozen complete post-exposure conversation context, whereas CPB learners answered only from the frozen Explicit LTM.

\[
70\times7=490
\]

\[
70\times49=3{,}430
\]

\subsubsection{Evaluation Measures}
\label{subsubsec:study2-measures}

The two Study 2 subquestions share the same set of basic outcome measures. The five independent runs of each assessment question within the same learner and material condition are first used to calculate a question-level mean Checklist Score, run-to-run standard deviation (SD), and mean answer word count. Checklist Score is the assessment-performance outcome, SD describes repeated-run stability, and response word count is retained only as a descriptive answer characteristic. It is used to determine whether different conditions produce systematic differences in response length and is not interpreted as learning performance itself.

For question $q$, learner condition $c$, material condition $m$, and run $j$, let the Checklist Score be $S_{q,c,m,j}$. The question-level mean Checklist Score is

\[
\bar S_{q,c,m}
=
\frac{1}{5}
\sum_{j=1}^{5}
S_{q,c,m,j}.
\]

$\bar S_{q,c,m}$ represents the mean assessment performance on question $q$ under a given learner and material condition. All higher-level summaries are based on this question-level mean, including the overall mean across all 49 questions, lesson-level means across the seven instructional materials, the mean across the 42 Independent Questions, and the mean across the seven cross-round Integrative Questions. Study 2 therefore uses the consistent aggregation order

\[\small
\text{Run-level Score}
\rightarrow
\text{Question-level Mean}
\rightarrow
\text{Lesson / Question-Type / Overall Summary}.
\]

Stability across the five independent runs of the same question is expressed using the question-level run-to-run SD:

\[
SD_{q,c,m}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{
\sum_{j=1}^{5}
\left(
S_{q,c,m,j}-\bar S_{q,c,m}
\right)^2
}{
5-1
}
}.
\]

This calculation uses the sample standard deviation with $5-1=4$ degrees of freedom. Because SD and Checklist Score use the same scale, SD directly expresses variation in performance across repeated generations under the same experimental condition. Lower SD indicates more stable performance across the five runs of a question, whereas higher SD indicates greater run-to-run variability. Question-level SD is used only as an intermediate quantity. The only reported SD summary is the \textbf{Overall Mean Question-Level Run SD}, which weights all 49 questions equally:

\[
\overline{SD}_{c,m}
=
\frac{1}{Q}
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
SD_{q,c,m},
\qquad Q=49.
\]

Let $\mathcal Q_\ell$ denote the seven assessment questions associated with Lesson $\ell$, comprising six Independent Questions and one Integrative Question. To preserve equal weighting of question-level quantities, the lesson-level Mean Checklist Score is

\[
\bar S_{\ell,c,m}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal Q_\ell|}
\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q_\ell}
\bar S_{q,c,m},
\qquad
|\mathcal Q_\ell|=7.
\]

The lesson-level Mean Checklist Score therefore retains the Run $\rightarrow$ Question $\rightarrow$ Lesson aggregation order rather than pooling raw run-level observations directly. Run-to-run stability is reported only through the Overall Mean Question-Level Run SD across all questions; a separate lesson-level SD is neither calculated nor reported.

In addition to performance and stability, student-answer word count is recorded as a descriptive response characteristic. Let $W_{q,c,m,j}$ denote the answer word count for run $j$. The question-level Mean Answer Words is

\[
\bar W_{q,c,m}
=
\frac{1}{5}
\sum_{j=1}^{5}W_{q,c,m,j}.
\]

Lesson-level and overall Mean Answer Words are

\[
\bar W_{\ell,c,m}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal Q_\ell|}
\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q_\ell}\bar W_{q,c,m},
\qquad
\overline W_{c,m}
=
\frac{1}{Q}
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\bar W_{q,c,m}.
\]

These measures support checks of whether persona or CPB conditions systematically produce longer or shorter answers and whether score differences are accompanied by changes in response verbosity. They are not interpreted as direct measures of retained knowledge, learning effectiveness, or cognitive load.

\paragraph{SQ1---Within-Representation Performance Differentiation.}

SQ1 uses distracted materials only and examines whether the learner-condition manipulation produces systematic performance differentiation within each representation. The expected ordered condition sequences for Prompt and CPB are

\[
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{Prompt}}
=
(\mathrm{NT},\mathrm{Moderate},\mathrm{High}),
\]

\[
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{CPB}}
=
(\mathrm{Zero},\mathrm{Low},\mathrm{Medium},\mathrm{High}).
\]

Both representations use the same basic outcome measures. Overall Mean Checklist Score, Overall Mean Question-Level Run SD, and Mean Answer Words are reported, together with Mean Checklist Score and Mean Answer Words for L01--L07, Independent Questions, and Integrative Questions. Run SD is reported only as the Overall Mean Question-Level Run SD that weights all 49 questions equally; it is not repeated at lesson or question-type level. Mean Answer Words describes response form only and is not used to judge learning effectiveness.

Because a Lesson is an instructional unit with a clear content boundary and each lesson-level mean aggregates the question-level means of its seven questions, Lesson OCR is the primary ordering measure for SQ1. Let the ordered condition sequence for representation $g\in\{\mathrm{Prompt},\mathrm{CPB}\}$ be

\[
\mathcal C_g
=
(c_{g,1},c_{g,2},\ldots,c_{g,K_g}).
\]

Its Lesson-level Ordering Consistency Rate is

\[
OCR^{\mathrm{Lesson}}_{g,m}
=
\frac{1}{7}
\sum_{\ell=1}^{7}
\mathbb I
\left(
\bar S_{\ell,c_{g,1},m}
\ge
\bar S_{\ell,c_{g,2},m}
\ge
\cdots
\ge
\bar S_{\ell,c_{g,K_g},m}
\right).
\]

This measure is the proportion of the seven instructional materials whose lesson-level Mean Checklist Scores satisfy the representation-specific expected ordering. A higher Lesson OCR indicates that the ordering is preserved across more materials. Because the measure contains only seven lessons, it changes in increments of $1/7=14.29$ percentage points and must be interpreted alongside the realised score differences rather than as independent evidence of statistical significance.

Because non-strict ordering counts completely equal means as satisfying the ordering, the Lesson-level Complete Equality Rate is also reported:

\[
ER^{\mathrm{Lesson}}_{g,m}
=
\frac{1}{7}
\sum_{\ell=1}^{7}
\mathbb I
\left(
\max_{c\in\mathcal C_g}\bar S_{\ell,c,m}
-
\min_{c\in\mathcal C_g}\bar S_{\ell,c,m}
\le \epsilon
\right),
\]

where the fixed numerical tolerance is $\epsilon=10^{-9}$. High OCR with low ER is more consistent with ordered differentiation involving actual score separation; high OCR with high ER indicates that the observed ordering may be produced mainly by exact ties.

To examine whether lesson-level aggregation conceals item-level heterogeneity, Question OCR and Question ER are calculated analogously:

\[
OCR^{\mathrm{Question}}_{g,m}
=
\frac{1}{Q}
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\mathbb I
\left(
\bar S_{q,c_{g,1},m}
\ge
\bar S_{q,c_{g,2},m}
\ge
\cdots
\ge
\bar S_{q,c_{g,K_g},m}
\right),
\qquad Q=49,
\]

\[
ER^{\mathrm{Question}}_{g,m}
=
\frac{1}{Q}
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\mathbb I
\left(
\max_{c\in\mathcal C_g}\bar S_{q,c,m}
-
\min_{c\in\mathcal C_g}\bar S_{q,c,m}
\le \epsilon
\right).
\]

Question OCR and ER are supporting measures that describe item-level ordering, ties, and local reversals within the materials; they do not replace the primary Lesson OCR and ER. Mean Checklist Score and Mean Answer Words are also reported separately for Independent and Integrative Questions to describe whether performance and response form vary with question structure. Run SD is not additionally reported at question-type level.

To quantify the realised score differences underlying the ordering, SQ1 also reports lesson-level paired score contrasts. For a lower-severity condition $a$ and a higher-severity condition $b$ under the same material condition $m$, the paired difference for each lesson is

\[
d_{\ell,a,b,m}
=
\bar S_{\ell,a,m}
-
\bar S_{\ell,b,m},
\]

and the equally weighted summary across the seven lessons is

\[
\Delta_{a,b,m}
=
\frac{1}{7}
\sum_{\ell=1}^{7}
d_{\ell,a,b,m}.
\]

The seven lesson-specific differences $d_{\ell,a,b,m}$ are the paired analysis units for calculating the overall contrast and paired lesson bootstrap; they are not individually tabulated in the Results. Actual lesson means and their ordering across lessons are reported through the lesson-level score figure and Lesson OCR/ER. Table 4.RQ2-3 reports only the equally weighted overall contrast $\Delta_{a,b,m}$ and its 95\% confidence interval, quantifying the overall magnitude and uncertainty of the condition difference.

A positive value means that the higher-severity condition has a lower lesson-level Mean Checklist Score. The Prompt branch reports $\Delta_{\mathrm{NT-Mod}}$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{Mod-High}}$, and $\Delta_{\mathrm{NT-High}}$. The CPB branch reports $\Delta_{\mathrm{Zero-Low}}$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{Low-Medium}}$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{Medium-High}}$, and $\Delta_{\mathrm{Zero-High}}$. All SQ1 contrasts are calculated only under distracted materials and are reported as raw Checklist Score differences with 95\% confidence intervals, rather than percentages relative to baseline.

The 95\% confidence intervals use a paired lesson bootstrap. Seven Lessons are sampled with replacement while all learner conditions remain paired within each selected Lesson, and $\Delta_{a,b,m}$ is recalculated. After 10,000 repetitions, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution are used as the interval endpoints. This procedure expresses contrast uncertainty across the seven instructional materials. Because the number of Lessons is limited, the interval is interpreted jointly with Lesson OCR/ER and the realised lesson means rather than used alone to support a mechanistic conclusion.

SQ1 concludes only whether the two representations separately produce systematic performance differentiation. Absolute Prompt and CPB scores are not used directly to judge comparative representational validity. Consistency with ADHD-related cognitive hypotheses is evaluated separately through the process-sensitive measures in SQ2.

\paragraph{SQ2---ADHD-Theory-Consistent Process Sensitivity.}

\textbf{Controlled-distraction component.}

This component uses paired performance under clean and distracted materials for all seven learner conditions to test whether adding task-irrelevant distractors produces a distraction-associated change in performance. For question $q$ and learner condition $c$, the question-level \textbf{Raw distraction cost} is

\[
\Delta D_{q,c}
=
\bar S_{q,c,\mathrm{clean}}
-
\bar S_{q,c,\mathrm{distracted}}.
\]

Thus, $\Delta D_{q,c}>0$ indicates that performance is lower under distracted materials, a value close to zero indicates little change between material conditions, and a negative value indicates higher performance under distracted materials.

For material condition $m$, the overall Mean Checklist Score for learner condition $c$ is

\[
\bar S_{c,m}
=
\frac{1}{Q}
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\bar S_{q,c,m}.
\]

The Overall Raw distraction cost is the equally weighted mean across all eligible questions and is also equal to the difference between the overall clean and distracted mean scores:

\[
\overline{\Delta D}_{c}
=
\frac{1}{Q}
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
\Delta D_{q,c}
=
\bar S_{c,\mathrm{clean}}
-
\bar S_{c,\mathrm{distracted}}.
\]

Its 95\% confidence interval is calculated from the 49 matched question-level clean-minus-distracted differences using a two-sided $t$-interval:

\[
\overline{\Delta D}_{c}
\;\pm\;
t_{0.975,Q-1}
\frac{s_{\Delta D_c}}{\sqrt{Q}},
\qquad Q=49,
\]

where $s_{\Delta D_c}$ is the sample SD of the question-level Raw distraction costs for learner condition $c$.

The Results first use a Raw-distraction-cost figure to show the realised clean-minus-distracted performance change for all seven learner conditions. Bars represent the Overall Raw distraction cost across the 49 questions, black error bars show the 95\% confidence interval above, and grey points show lesson-level Raw distraction costs for L01--L07. Prompt and CPB are displayed in separate panels with horizontal scales appropriate to their respective data ranges so that the Prompt changes close to zero are not compressed by the larger CPB costs. The formal representation-relative comparison is made through Additional distraction cost.

To avoid interpreting a material-condition change shared by both representations as ADHD-related process sensitivity, a reference condition is defined within each representation. Prompt-ADHD---Moderate and Prompt-ADHD---High are target conditions in the Prompt branch and Prompt-NT is the reference. CPB Low, Medium, and High are target conditions in the CPB branch and CPB Zero is the reference.

For any target condition $t$ and its corresponding reference $r$, the question-level \textbf{Additional distraction cost versus reference} is

\[
C^D_{q,t}
=
\Delta D_{q,t}
-
\Delta D_{q,r}.
\]

$C^D_{q,t}>0$ indicates that the target condition has a larger distraction-associated performance loss than its representation-specific reference, a value close to zero indicates similar distraction sensitivity, and a negative value indicates that the target has a lower Raw distraction cost than the reference. The Overall Additional distraction cost is

\[
\overline{C^D}_{t}
=
\frac{1}{Q}
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}
C^D_{q,t}.
\]

Its 95\% confidence interval uses a two-sided $t$-interval over the paired question-level $C^D_{q,t}$ values:

\[
\overline{C^D}_{t}
\;\pm\;
t_{0.975,Q-1}
\frac{s_{C^D_t}}{\sqrt{Q}}.
\]

Here, $s_{C^D_t}$ is the sample SD of the question-level Additional costs between the target and reference. Prompt-NT and CPB Zero are the reference conditions for their respective representations and are not listed as target contrasts in the Additional-distraction-cost table; no Additional-cost confidence interval or lesson coverage is reported for them.

To examine whether Additional distraction cost retains the same direction across instructional materials, the Raw distraction cost for each lesson is first calculated as

\[
\Delta D_{\ell,c}
=
\bar S_{\ell,c,\mathrm{clean}}
-
\bar S_{\ell,c,\mathrm{distracted}}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal Q_\ell|}
\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q_\ell}
\Delta D_{q,c},
\]

followed by the lesson-level Additional cost of the target relative to the reference:

\[
C^D_{\ell,t}
=
\Delta D_{\ell,t}
-
\Delta D_{\ell,r},
\]

\[
\mathrm{Coverage}_{t}
=
\frac{
\#\left\{\ell:C^D_{\ell,t}>0\right\}
}{7}.
\]

Coverage is the number or proportion of the seven instructional materials in which the target condition has a positive Additional distraction cost. Higher coverage indicates that additional distraction sensitivity retains the expected direction across more materials rather than being driven principally by a small number of lessons. Coverage describes directional consistency across lessons and does not replace the Overall Additional cost or its 95\% confidence interval.

\textbf{Processing-demand component.}

This component examines whether question performance changes systematically as instructional processing demand increases. The primary analysis is restricted to the 42 Independent Questions because each maps to one explicit source Teaching Round and its frozen Processing Demand Bits (PDB). Cross-round Integrative Questions involve multiple Teaching Rounds and therefore have no unique single-round PDB; they are excluded from the primary continuous-demand analysis.

The PDB analysis primarily uses clean-material performance. Because the clean condition contains no distractor-triggered information loss, it reduces confounding by Attention-related loss in the PDB--score relationship and more directly examines behavioural sensitivity associated with instructional processing demand. Distracted-material performance profiles are retained only as a descriptive directional check of whether the relationship remains visible when distractor-triggered and processing-demand constraints co-occur.

The 42 Independent Questions are first ordered from low to high by source-round PDB, and question-level Mean Checklist Score profiles are plotted for each learner condition. This visualisation supports inspection of whether higher processing demand is accompanied by a systematic decline in performance and whether that relationship changes with persona intensity or CPB constraint severity.

The primary association measure for processing-demand sensitivity is the PDB--score slope and its 95\% confidence interval. For each learner condition, the following model is fitted under clean materials:

\[
\bar S_q
=
\alpha
+
\beta_{\mathrm{PDB},100}
\left(
\frac{PDB_q}{100}
\right)
+
\varepsilon_q.
\]

$PDB_q/100$ scales source-round processing demand into 100-bit units. $\beta_{\mathrm{PDB},100}$ therefore represents the estimated change in Mean Checklist Score associated with a 100-bit increase in PDB, rather than the unscaled change associated with one bit. A value close to zero indicates little evident linear relationship between performance and PDB. $\beta_{\mathrm{PDB},100}<0$ indicates that higher-demand source rounds are associated with lower question performance, with more negative values indicating stronger performance sensitivity to processing demand.

As in the controlled-distraction component, representation-relative interpretation uses internal reference-adjusted slope contrasts. The following quantities all refer to clean-material estimates:

\[
C^{\beta}_{s}
=
\beta_{\mathrm{Prompt}\text{-}\mathrm{ADHD}\ s,\mathrm{PDB},100}
-
\beta_{\mathrm{Prompt}\text{-}\mathrm{NT},\mathrm{PDB},100},
\]

\[
C^{\beta}_{k}
=
\beta_{\mathrm{CPB}\ k,\mathrm{PDB},100}
-
\beta_{\mathrm{CPB\ Zero},\mathrm{PDB},100}.
\]

A more negative $C^\beta$ indicates that an ADHD-related or constrained condition has a stronger negative PDB--performance relationship than the reference within the same representation.

The 95\% confidence interval for a Raw PDB--score slope is calculated using the ordinary least-squares slope standard error:

\[
\hat\beta_{\mathrm{PDB},100}
\;\pm\;
t_{0.975,Q-2}\,
SE\!\left(\hat\beta_{\mathrm{PDB},100}\right),
\]

where $Q=42$ in the primary analysis. The 95\% confidence interval for a reference-adjusted slope contrast is calculated by paired question-level bootstrap. The 42 matched Independent Questions are sampled with replacement 10,000 times; in each sample the target and reference PDB slopes and their difference are re-estimated, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution define the interval. A fixed random seed is used to make the result reproducible.

The clean-material PDB--score slope and its 95\% confidence interval first quantify the direction, magnitude, and uncertainty of the PDB--performance relationship within each condition. The reference-adjusted slope contrast and its 95\% confidence interval then test whether the target condition's PDB relationship exceeds the baseline trend of its representation-specific reference. Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$ is a supporting association measure used to determine whether the observed relationship also has a monotonic direction without assuming linearity. A negative $\rho$ indicates that performance tends to decrease as PDB increases, and values closer to $-1$ indicate a more consistent negative monotonic relationship. A reference-adjusted $\rho$ is not defined separately.

The confidence intervals based on 49 or 42 matched questions describe uncertainty within the frozen task-item set; they do not support inference to a broader population of instructional materials or to the human ADHD population. The controlled-distraction analysis also reports expected-direction lesson coverage, whereas SQ1 uses lesson-level contrasts, to describe whether observed directions are retained across the seven frozen materials.

CPB Low, Medium, and High jointly vary Attention-trigger probability and WM capacity. Study 2 therefore tests whether the two constraints, when enabled together, produce directionally consistent process-sensitive behavioural evidence; it does not estimate their independent causal contributions.

Independent and Integrative question performance is retained as a question-structure summary for SQ1. The controlled-distraction component uses an equally weighted summary across all 49 questions and is not further divided by question type in the primary analysis. Because Integrative Questions cannot map uniquely to a single source-round PDB, they are excluded from the continuous processing-demand analysis.

\subsection{Study 3---Multidimensional Representation Performance}

\subsubsection{Research Question and Subquestions}
\label{subsubsec:study3-rq}

Study 3 addresses RQ3 by adding Language Ability and Big-Five prompt dimensions at the response stage of the Study 2 learner conditions. Learner characteristics are not isolated from one another in real learners, but the A1--B2 conditions in this study are treated only as controlled attribute-prompt additions rather than as an independent validation of Big-Five characteristics or complete multidimensional learner fidelity. Study 3 therefore describes how these prompt additions change assessment outcomes and examines whether the constraint-related aggregate patterns already established for CPB in Study 2 remain observable. RQ3 is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ3---Constraint-Pattern Retention under Attribute Additions:} When Language Ability and Big-Five prompt dimensions are added at the response stage, how do assessment outcomes change, and do CPB's previously established constraint-related aggregate patterns remain observable?
\end{quote}

RQ3 contains two connected levels of analysis. SQ1 describes changes in assessment outcomes caused by the A1--B2 prompt additions relative to the Study 2 baseline. SQ2 examines whether the distraction- and processing-demand-related aggregate patterns observed in Study 2 remain visible after those additions. Neither subquestion validates complete multidimensional fidelity or independently tests whether the Big-Five characteristics are faithfully expressed.

\paragraph{SQ1---Baseline-Relative Behavioural Change.}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{How do the assessment-performance patterns of the Study 2 learner conditions change after Language Ability and Big-Five profile dimensions are introduced?}
\end{quote}

Adding Language Ability and Big-Five characteristics can reasonably change response expression, content organisation, and final scores; behavioural change itself is therefore not defined in advance as representation failure. SQ1 instead examines whether the added profile dimensions change the original answer-performance patterns and whether the within-representation performance structure identified in Study 2 remains distinguishable under the more complex learner descriptions.

\paragraph{SQ2---Retention of Constraint-Related Process Patterns.}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Do the distraction- and processing-demand-related aggregate patterns established in Study 2 remain observable after response-stage attribute prompts are added?}
\end{quote}

SQ2 treats A1--B2 as response-stage prompt perturbations and examines whether distraction- and processing-demand-related performance patterns remain observable after these prompts are introduced. Pattern retention here is not equivalent to validation of the psychological fidelity of the added attributes.

Overall, Study 3 first describes how response-stage attribute additions change assessment outcomes and then checks whether CPB's previously established constraint-related aggregate patterns remain observable.

\paragraph{SQ1---Baseline-Relative Behavioural Change.}

SQ1 uses distracted materials only and reports results at two levels. The first level retains Mean Checklist Score, Overall Mean Question-Level Run SD, and Mean Answer Words for every learner-condition $\times$ profile cell, allowing the effect of A1--B2 on specific learner conditions to be identified. The second level forms representation $\times$ profile summaries by taking equal-weight means across Prompt Moderate/High and CPB Low/Medium/High within each A1--B2 profile. The profile-grouped Overall Mean Checklist Score and its matched Study 2 change provide the overall profile-level summary; learner-condition-specific score changes indicate whether that overall result is driven by a particular condition. Lesson-specific score changes are retained in the experimental outputs as diagnostic records and are not separately reported in the main results. Profile-grouped run SD and Mean Answer Words and their changes are supporting or descriptive evidence.

Every Study 3 distracted-material cell is matched to the result from the same learner condition and aggregation level under distracted materials in Study 2, using exactly the same aggregation order. The material condition in the following SQ1 equations is fixed at $m=\mathrm{distracted}$. For $X\in\{\bar S,\overline W\}$, the lesson-specific baseline-relative change is

\[
\Delta X_{\ell,c,p,m}
=
X^{(3)}_{\ell,c,p,m}
-
X^{(2)}_{\ell,c,m}.
\]

The overall delta is the equally weighted mean of the seven lesson-specific deltas:

\[
\Delta X_{c,p,m}
=
\frac{1}{7}
\sum_{\ell=1}^{7}
\Delta X_{\ell,c,p,m}.
\]

$\Delta\bar S_{c,p,m}$ describes the overall score change for a specific learner condition under profile $p$, and $\Delta\bar S_{\ell,c,p,m}$ further describes how that change is distributed across L01--L07. Both are supporting measures for interpreting the profile-grouped result.

Run-to-run stability does not use a lesson-specific delta. Instead, the Overall Mean Question-Level Run SD across all questions is compared directly between the two studies:

\[
\Delta\overline{SD}_{c,p,m}
=
\overline{SD}^{(3)}_{c,p,m}
-
\overline{SD}^{(2)}_{c,m}.
\]

A positive $\Delta\bar S$ means that the Study 3 Mean Checklist Score after adding the profile dimensions is higher than the matched Study 2 condition, and a negative value means that it is lower. This measure describes the direction and magnitude of performance change, but an increase or decrease in score does not itself indicate that the representation is better or worse. A positive $\Delta\overline{SD}$ indicates greater score variation across the five runs and hence lower run-to-run stability; a negative value indicates more concentrated repeated-run outcomes and higher stability. A positive $\Delta\overline W$ indicates longer answers overall, and a negative value indicates shorter answers. It describes only response length and expressive form and is not interpreted as knowledge gain, learning effectiveness, or profile fidelity.

To use A1--B2 as the primary profile-grouping factor, define

\[
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{Prompt}}
=
\{\mathrm{Moderate},\mathrm{High}\},
\qquad
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{CPB}}
=
\{\mathrm{Low},\mathrm{Medium},\mathrm{High}\}.
\]

For the overall outcome $Y\in\{\bar S,\overline{SD},\overline W\}$, the representation $\times$ profile summary, matched Study 2 representation baseline, and their difference are

\[
Y^{(3)}_{r,p,m}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal C_r|}
\sum_{c\in\mathcal C_r}
Y^{(3)}_{c,p,m},
\]

\[
Y^{(2)}_{r,m}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal C_r|}
\sum_{c\in\mathcal C_r}
Y^{(2)}_{c,m},
\]

\[
\Delta Y_{r,p,m}
=
Y^{(3)}_{r,p,m}
-
Y^{(2)}_{r,m}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal C_r|}
\sum_{c\in\mathcal C_r}
\Delta Y_{c,p,m}.
\]

Each A1--B2 profile therefore produces one Prompt summary and one CPB summary. For $Y=\overline{SD}$, the summary is the equal-weight mean of the Overall Mean Question-Level Run SD values for the learner conditions, rather than a pooled SD recalculated after combining runs from different conditions. Profile-grouped absolute outcomes and deltas are reported together: the former describes the overall level after the profile is added, and the latter describes change relative to the matched Study 2 representation baseline.

Within-representation performance structure directly reuses the Study 2 definitions of OCR and ER. OCR and ER are not averaged across learner conditions; instead, they are calculated directly from the relevant condition ordering and equality within each representation $\times$ profile. Prompt tests Moderate $\ge$ High, and CPB tests Low $\ge$ Medium $\ge$ High. Lesson OCR/ER is the primary structure evidence, whereas Question OCR/ER describes item-level heterogeneity. Study 2 baselines are recalculated using the same learner subsets and are fixed to distracted materials so that they correspond directly to every A1--B2 profile.

Profile-specific changes in OCR and ER are reported in percentage points:

\[
\Delta OCR^{a}_{r,p,m}
=
100\left(
OCR^{(3),a}_{r,p,m}
-
OCR^{(2),a}_{r,m}
\right),
\]

\[
\Delta ER^{a}_{r,p,m}
=
100\left(
ER^{(3),a}_{r,p,m}
-
ER^{(2),a}_{r,m}
\right).
\]

OCR is interpreted jointly with ER and the realised Mean Checklist Scores. High OCR accompanied by high ER indicates that the expected non-increasing ordering is formed to a substantial extent by equal scores rather than clear condition separation. Profile-specific score separation uses the lesson-level contrast procedure from Study 2 to examine whether the ordering represented by OCR has a realised score magnitude.

SQ1 therefore reports 20 learner-condition $\times$ profile baseline-relative deltas and eight representation $\times$ profile overall summaries. The former locate changes in specific learner conditions; the latter compare the overall effects of A1--B2 on Prompt and CPB outcomes. Performance change between clean and distracted materials is analysed only in the controlled-distraction component of SQ2.

\subsection{Models and Generation Settings}

The purpose is to avoid having one model simultaneously perform learner simulation, mechanism measurement, and outcome evaluation within the same experimental chain. For readability and consistent naming, the DeepSeek model used for simulated-student answering and Knowledge Encoding is referred to throughout the thesis as \textbf{DeepSeek V4-Flash}. In the implementation, all requests were sent through the official DeepSeek API using the compatible model identifier \texttt{deepseek-chat}. The product name and API request identifier are not at the same naming level: DeepSeek V4-Flash identifies the model version used in this study, whereas \texttt{deepseek-chat} is a compatibility alias maintained by the service provider. DeepSeek's official update record states that the model underlying this alias changes with service versions; in the DeepSeek V4 API migration note, \texttt{deepseek-chat} is mapped to the non-thinking mode of DeepSeek V4-Flash. The main text therefore uses ``DeepSeek V4-Flash'' as the consistent readable name while retaining the actual request identifier \texttt{deepseek-chat} in the model configuration and reproducibility records \cite{deepseek2026api}.

DeepSeek V4-Flash has two distinct roles in this study that share the same model configuration. First, it is the student model that generates every assessment answer for Prompt and CPB learners. Prompt learners answer from the frozen complete post-exposure classroom context, whereas CPB learners answer only from the Explicit LTM produced during learning. Second, it is the Knowledge Encoder shared by all CPB conditions, converting the Available Input that reaches the Encoder in one Teaching Round into a brief round-level Explicit LTM entry. Using the same base model for both roles avoids interpreting cross-model capability differences as differences between the Prompt and CPB representations. The two stages nevertheless use separate frozen system prompts and input boundaries, so Knowledge Encoding and assessment answering are not part of the same model call.

DeepSeek requests for simulated-student answering use \texttt{temperature=0.0} and a maximum output of 1,200 tokens. The Knowledge Encoder also uses \texttt{temperature=0.0}, with a maximum output of 500 tokens. All requests use non-streaming generation, a 180-second timeout, and at most three request retries. The Encoder is instructed to summarise only the classroom information it actually receives, not to add external knowledge, and to return one plain-text memory entry without a heading, list, or supplementary commentary. If no Available Input remains after Attention and WM processing in a Teaching Round, the Encoder is not called and no LTM entry is generated. For identical Available Input, Encoder prompt, and model configuration, the system reuses a previously generated encoding through a content-addressed cache to prevent repeated generation from introducing additional differences across equivalent CPB conditions.

The setting \texttt{temperature=0.0} is used to reduce generation variation unrelated to the experimental manipulation, but it is not treated as a mathematical guarantee of deterministic output. DeepSeek requests do not pass a fixed generation seed, and the remote API service may still be affected by changes in backend implementation and inference environment. Studies 2 and 3 therefore execute five independent runs for every learner condition and estimate Mean Checklist Score, sample SD, and other run-to-run variability measures from the stored outputs. The treatment of repeated runs and statistical uncertainty is described in Section~3.6.3.

In addition to the generative models, GPT-2 small is used as the frozen offline probability estimator for PDB. The study uses the OpenAI GPT-2 small model released through Hugging Face and its corresponding tokenizer. Model weights, tokenizer, and inference configuration remain fixed for all PDB calculations. GPT-2 does not participate in student answering or LTM generation. It acts as a measurement instrument that calculates cumulative surprisal separately for each Teaching Round. Model context is reset at the start of every round and initialised with an unscored boundary token; the two or three sentences within the round share a continuous context. This configuration makes PDB a frozen material-level annotation rather than a variable generated dynamically by a particular simulated-student run. The PDB formula, rationale for model selection, and estimator-sensitivity analysis are described in Sections~3.2.2 and~3.5.1.

Encoding Semantic Recall (ESR) and E2ESR Source-to-LTM semantic correspondence in Study 1 use the official BERTScore implementation with \path{microsoft/deberta-xlarge-mnli} as the contextual-embedding model \cite{zhang2019bertscore}. Every comparison reports the Recall component, disables inverse-document-frequency weighting (\texttt{idf=False}), and enables baseline rescaling. This model is used only for offline embedding-based semantic-correspondence analysis of stored text. It does not participate in Attention, WM, Knowledge Encoding, or assessment answering, and it does not perform proposition-level judgements of knowledge retention. The reference--candidate direction and measure interpretation are defined in the Study 1 Evaluation Measures.

Every formal student answer is scored by the frozen \texttt{qwen3.7-max} criterion-level LLM Judge. The Judge uses \texttt{temperature=0.0} with a maximum output of 2,400 tokens and returns the \texttt{Correct}, \texttt{Absent}, or \texttt{Contradicted} label for every atomic checklist criterion in structured JSON. The Judge request contains only the frozen question, reference answer, atomic checklist criteria, source evidence, and student answer. Student identity, representation condition, material condition, Attention and WM processing logs, PDB, Explicit LTM, and run number are excluded. The model produces criterion-level semantic labels only; output-schema validation and aggregation into the 0--10 Checklist Score are performed by deterministic local code. Because the preliminary model comparison and three-round stability test supported the repeat-measurement stability of \texttt{qwen3.7-max}, each student answer in formal Studies 2 and 3 receives one frozen Judge call. Study 1 produces no assessment answers and therefore uses no Judge scoring. Judge selection, stability, and agreement with human scoring are reported in Section~\ref{subsec:judge-validation}.

GPT-2, BERTScore, and the Qwen Judge provide no information to the simulated student, and their outputs are not fed back into student generation. Likewise, DeepSeek cannot access reference answers, checklist criteria, or Judge outputs when generating student answers. The experimental pipeline therefore maintains functional separation among student generation, processing-demand annotation, semantic analysis, and outcome evaluation.

\subsection{Experiment Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:experiment-reproducibility}

Reproducibility was addressed through frozen experimental inputs, controlled stochastic mechanisms, persistent model outputs, and repeatable downstream analysis. Because the experiments relied on remotely hosted LLM APIs, the study does not assume exact-output reproducibility. Instead, the goal was to ensure that each reported result could be traced to a fixed experimental configuration and reproduced analytically from the stored outputs.

All formal experiments used the same frozen instructional and assessment resources, including the seven lessons, 83 Teaching Rounds, 49 assessment questions, source mappings, reference answers, checklist criteria, prompts, and Study-specific configurations. Relevant files and configurations were versioned and recorded with content hashes. Learner conditions, material conditions, repetition counts, Attention probabilities, Working-Memory capacities, and run-specific seeds were fixed before execution, allowing every output to be linked to its corresponding Study, condition, run, and input version.

Three sources of stochasticity were treated separately. CPB Attention triggering was part of the experimental manipulation and used frozen run seeds, so the same registered configuration reproduced the same Attention trajectory. Student answering and Knowledge Encoding used fixed generation settings, including \texttt{temperature = 0}, but no claim of deterministic regeneration is made because remote API execution remains outside experimental control; repeated runs were therefore used to characterise run-to-run variation. Bootstrap seeds were independent of both processes and controlled only offline statistical resampling.

Accordingly, persisted outputs rather than repeated API calls formed the primary reproducibility record. Each run retained the relevant classroom context or CPB processing logs, Available Input, Explicit LTM, assessment answers, criterion-level Judge outputs, question-level scores, and associated metadata. Identical CPB Encoding inputs reused the same cached Encoding result, preventing equivalent learning states from diverging through repeated API calls. Run manifests, output hashes, and integrity audits were used to verify that the registered runs, answers, processing records, memory states, and Judge results were complete before inclusion in analysis. Study 3 additionally verified the correspondence between matched CPB trajectories and their Study 2 baselines.

All statistical analyses were performed on these frozen outputs using fixed analysis configurations and version-recorded software dependencies. Bootstrap procedures used prespecified resampling units and fixed analysis seeds, allowing reported confidence intervals and derived statistics to be recomputed from the same stored data. Reproducibility in this study therefore refers to \textbf{reconstructing the experimental procedure, tracing results to frozen inputs and persisted outputs, and repeating downstream analyses}, rather than guaranteeing that future calls to the same remote LLM API will reproduce identical text.

\subsection{Ethical Scope and Interpretation}
\label{subsec:ethical-scope}

This study used only simulated learners and did not involve real students with ADHD or any other neurodivergent condition. The experimental conditions should therefore be interpreted as computational models for controlled hypothesis testing rather than representations of individual learners or clinically valid models of ADHD. The selected Attention- and Working-Memory-related mechanisms were motivated by group-level evidence reviewed in Section~2.4, but neither mechanism is assumed to characterise all individuals with ADHD.

The CPB learners were not provided with an ADHD label, diagnostic description, or instruction to behave as an ADHD student. ADHD-related differences were instead operationalised through predefined information-processing constraints. This design reduces direct reliance on label-conditioned or stereotype-based role playing when testing the CPB mechanisms. It does not, however, eliminate all possible bias: the underlying LLM may still reflect biases from pretraining, and the selected mechanisms remain study-specific abstractions of a much more heterogeneous condition. Persona-prompted ADHD learners were retained only as a comparison representation and should likewise not be interpreted as clinically representative profiles.

Accordingly, behavioural outcomes in this thesis are used only to evaluate the experimental representation mechanisms under controlled conditions. Lower assessment scores, greater distraction sensitivity, or stronger processing-demand effects are not treated as diagnostic markers or as evidence that a learner is ``more ADHD-like''. The simulations are not intended for diagnosis, learner classification, or direct educational decision-making. Claims are therefore limited to the computational behaviour, traceability, and theory-consistent patterns observed within the frozen experimental setting.
